A senior pet is not an adult pet with grey hair. The body has measurably less reserve, organ function declines, and the symptom patterns that meant "monitor" at age 5 often mean "go in" at age 13.

This email is about resetting the triage card for senior pets and having the harder conversations with your primary vet before you're forced to.

## What counts as senior

Roughly:

- **Small-breed dogs (under 25 lb):** senior around age 10
- **Medium dogs (25–50 lb):** senior around age 8
- **Large dogs (50–80 lb):** senior around age 7
- **Giant breeds (80+ lb):** senior around age 6 (Great Danes, Newfoundlands, Saint Bernards age fast)
- **Cats:** senior around age 11; geriatric around age 15
- **Ferrets:** senior around age 4
- **Rabbits:** senior around age 6

AAHA's senior care guidelines suggest twice-yearly (rather than annual) wellness exams once a pet enters the senior bracket. The biological reason: in geriatric animals, six months is a meaningful change.

## How vital sign baselines shift

A senior pet's baseline is **not** the textbook adult range. Common shifts:

- **Heart rate:** May trend lower at rest in dogs (cardiac efficiency declines, but compensation occurs). Resting heart rate that climbs over time is a warning sign of heart disease or chronic pain.
- **Respiratory rate:** Sleeping RR > 30 in a cat (any age) is a red flag. In a senior cat with even subclinical heart disease, this becomes the single most important home metric. Track weekly if recommended.
- **Temperature:** Hypothermia becomes more common — seniors lose heat faster. < 99 °F is more concerning in a senior than an adult.
- **CRT and gum colour:** Slightly slower CRT can be normal in well-controlled chronic conditions; sudden changes still matter.

**Establish the new baseline at the start of senior years.** Take vitals weekly for the first month after your pet crosses into senior. That's your reference range going forward.

## ER threshold becomes more conservative

A young adult pet who skips one meal: monitor at home.

A 13-year-old cat who skips one meal: same-day vet call. Senior cats develop hepatic lipidosis dramatically faster than adults.

A young dog who limps and walks it off in an hour: monitor at home.

A 12-year-old dog who limps and walks it off in an hour: still call. Senior dogs have more structural reasons for sudden lameness — vertebral disc disease, soft-tissue sarcomas, splenic mass causing referred pain.

A general rule: **anything in the "monitor at home" column for an adult pet becomes "same-day vet" for a senior pet** if it persists more than 12 hours. The clinical reserve isn't there.

## New chronic-condition flags

Senior pets often develop chronic conditions whose flare-ups are themselves emergencies:

### Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

Very common in senior cats. A cat with diagnosed CKD who suddenly stops eating, vomits repeatedly, or shows weakness is in a **uremic crisis** until proven otherwise — ER visit.

### Diabetes mellitus

Diabetic pets can develop diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Symptoms: lethargy, vomiting, fruity-smelling breath, deep panting. ER NOW. Untreated DKA is rapidly fatal.

### Cushing's, Addison's, hyperthyroidism

Hormone-mediated chronic diseases destabilize fast. Owners of pets on chronic steroid replacement (Addison's), insulin (diabetes), or methimazole (cat hyperthyroidism) should keep printed dosing charts in the first-aid kit.

### Heart disease

A senior dog or cat with a known heart murmur on medication: sleeping respiratory rate > 30 sustained over an hour is the home red flag for decompensation. Track it.

### Cancer (including hemangiosarcoma)

In medium and large senior dogs, sudden weakness, pale gums, and collapse — even if it resolves — can be a splenic-tumor bleed. **Self-resolving collapse in a senior dog is still ER NOW.** It is one of the few patterns where "she got better on her own" is a worse sign, not a better one.

## The medication list problem

Senior pets are often on 3–6 medications. The ER vet at 11 p.m. doesn't have access to your primary vet's records. Carry:

- A printed current medication list with doses and last-administered times
- The most recent bloodwork (CBC, chemistry panel)
- Any known drug sensitivities or allergies
- The primary vet's name and phone number

Update this list every time medications change. Stick it inside the lid of your first-aid kit.

## The conversation with your primary vet

This is the one most owners avoid and most regret avoiding. Schedule it during a routine senior wellness visit, not during a crisis:

1. **Quality-of-life threshold.** What does your vet consider the threshold for hospice vs. continued treatment? What about euthanasia? Hearing this in a calm room beats trying to make the decision in a fluorescent-lit ER at 2 a.m.
2. **DNR / no-CPR question.** If your pet codes during a procedure, do you want CPR attempted? In senior pets with multiple conditions, the published survival rate after CPR is low (single-digit % per published studies). Many owners decide DNR is the more humane choice; some choose otherwise. The conversation should not happen for the first time during the emergency.
3. **Hospice options.** Some areas have veterinary hospice services that come to your home. Knowing the option exists, and the contact information, before you need it.
4. **In-home euthanasia.** Lap of Love and similar services offer in-home end-of-life care in most US metros. Cost is higher than in-clinic but the experience is profoundly different. Some pets — particularly those with severe vet-clinic anxiety — are clearly better served this way.

None of this is morbid. It's planning. Owners who have had this conversation are universally grateful they did, and uniformly say they wish they'd had it earlier. Owners who haven't are forced to make decisions while in shock.

## The senior-specific first-aid kit additions

Add to the kit you built in email #1:

- Current medication list (printed)
- Most recent bloodwork (printed)
- Primary vet contact, ER contact, in-home hospice/euthanasia contact (printed card)
- A note from your primary vet describing chronic conditions and current treatment plan — for the ER vet
- For dogs with mobility issues: a folded rear-end lift sling or strong towel for getting in/out of the car

## This week's homework

If your pet is in the senior bracket, schedule a 20-minute call with your primary vet specifically to update the medication list, take a fresh baseline of vitals, and have the quality-of-life conversation. Not during an annual exam — schedule it on its own time, with no other agenda.

Print everything. Put it in the first-aid kit.
