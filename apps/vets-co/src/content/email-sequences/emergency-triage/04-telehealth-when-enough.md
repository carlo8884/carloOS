The single most useful tool in the same-day-vet category is the one almost nobody uses: a $20–$50 telehealth call.

Three companies dominate the US pet-telehealth space — **Vetster**, **AskVet**, and **Pawp**. All three put a licensed vet (or vet tech in Pawp's case) on a video call within minutes, 24/7. None of them can prescribe controlled substances or perform diagnostic tests. All three can do something more valuable than people assume: tell you whether you're in the same-day or monitor-at-home bucket, and save you a $600 ER trip when you don't need one.

## When telehealth works

Telehealth is genuinely sufficient for:

- **A second opinion on whether to ER** at 11 p.m. (the #1 reason owners overpay for unnecessary ER visits)
- **Mild GI episodes** — one vomit, mild diarrhea, deciding whether to fast for 12 hours or escalate
- **Skin issues** — a hot spot, a mild ear shake, a single bump
- **Behavioral / training questions** that have a partial medical component
- **Mild itching or paw chewing** — environmental vs. allergic vs. flea
- **Medication questions** about a prescription your primary vet has already written
- **Post-surgery check-ins** ("is this swelling normal?")
- **Confirming "monitor at home"** when you can't sleep until someone in a white coat says so

A 20-minute call costs less than a tank of gas and saves the actual ER trip for actual emergencies.

## When telehealth does NOT work

Telehealth is **not** sufficient when:

- The triage card's ER NOW box has any item checked
- Your pet is in observable distress (open-mouth breathing in a cat, collapse, seizure)
- A diagnostic test (X-ray, bloodwork, ultrasound) is going to be needed regardless
- A controlled substance prescription is needed (the FDA limits this)
- The vet on the call says "you need to go in" — at which point, go in

A telehealth vet who tells you to go to the ER is, ironically, the best $30 you'll ever spend. They've used clinical judgement to confirm the trip is warranted, and the ER will sometimes accept their notes.

## The three services compared

### Vetster

The largest US pet telehealth network. Veterinarians, not vet techs. Can prescribe most non-controlled medications in states where the VCPR (veterinarian-client-patient relationship) allows. Pay-per-call, typically $50–$75. Worth it for anything where you might need a prescription.

### AskVet

Subscription model. Unlimited consults for a flat monthly fee (~$20/month). Vets (not techs). Cannot prescribe. Best fit if you have multiple pets or if you find yourself wanting to call frequently — the subscription pays for itself fast.

### Pawp

Hybrid model. The flagship feature is a $3,000 emergency fund per family per year, after a brief telehealth triage call where a tech says "yes, this needs ER care." Vet techs handle most calls, escalating to vets when needed. The emergency-fund piece is genuinely useful but read the terms carefully — there are exclusions and a one-emergency-per-year cap.

### A subscription vs. pay-per-call decision

Most owners are better off with **pay-per-call (Vetster) when needed** rather than a subscription, unless you've actually used telehealth twice in the past year. The math doesn't work otherwise — $240/year of subscription for a single 10-minute consult is worse than $50 once.

## What a good telehealth call looks like

1. **Have your data ready** before you dial:
   - The vital signs from your last home measurement (HR, RR, temp if you can take it)
   - Onset time and progression of symptoms
   - Anything your pet may have eaten or been exposed to
   - Your pet's weight, age, breed, and any chronic conditions
   - A list of current medications

2. **Have good lighting and a flat surface.** The vet may ask you to show gums, examine a wound, observe gait. A bathroom or kitchen counter beats a couch.

3. **Don't pre-diagnose.** Describe what you're seeing, not what you think it is. "My dog is panting more than usual and won't lie down" tells the vet more than "I think she has bloat."

4. **Take the recommendation seriously in both directions.** If the vet says "go in" — go. If the vet says "monitor 12 hours, here's what to watch for" — that is real medical advice, and ignoring it to ER anyway costs you money.

## How this fits the triage card

Map back to the card:

- **ER NOW box:** Skip telehealth, drive.
- **Same-day vet box:** Telehealth first is often appropriate, especially after-hours.
- **Monitor at home box:** Telehealth is overkill. Just watch.

## A note on the FDA VCPR rule

A "veterinarian-client-patient relationship" — the legal basis a vet needs to prescribe — has, until recently, generally required an in-person examination first. In **2025, the FDA finalized rule changes** that allow telehealth to establish a VCPR in some states for certain conditions. State laws still vary. The practical effect: telehealth vets can do more than they could two years ago, but not everything.
