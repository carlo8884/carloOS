Five emergencies per species that account for a disproportionate share of preventable pet deaths. Each one has a specific symptom pattern you can learn in two minutes — and recognition speed is the entire variable that decides outcome.

## Dogs — top 5

### 1. GDV / bloat (gastric dilatation-volvulus)

**Who:** Deep-chested large breeds — Great Danes, German Shepherds, Standard Poodles, Weimaraners, Setters. Less common in small breeds. Most common in large dogs over age 5 who eat one large meal a day.

**Symptoms:** A visibly bloated, hard abdomen. Repeated attempts to vomit with nothing coming up (unproductive retching). Restlessness, pacing, drooling. Pale gums. Collapse within hours if untreated.

**What to do:** ER NOW. Time matters in literal minutes. Untreated mortality is near 100%; treated, with timely surgery, survival is 70–80% per AAHA references. **Call ahead** so the ER has surgical staff ready.

**Prevention:** Slow-feeder bowl, multiple small meals, gastropexy in known at-risk breeds is worth discussing with your vet.

### 2. Antifreeze (ethylene glycol) ingestion

**Why it's deadly:** Ethylene glycol metabolizes to compounds that crystallize in the kidneys. Symptoms can look mild for the first 12 hours. By the time the animal seems clearly ill, kidney damage is often irreversible.

**Symptoms early:** Drunkenness, wobbling, vomiting, increased thirst and urination. **You will think it's getting better around hour 8–12** — this is the false-recovery window. Then severe kidney failure begins around hour 24–48.

**What to do:** ER NOW, the moment you suspect ingestion. Do not wait for symptoms. Antidote (4-methylpyrazole) is dramatically more effective the earlier it's given. If you can't reach an ER inside 1 hour, call Pet Poison Helpline first (855-764-7661).

**Prevention:** Store antifreeze sealed. Clean up driveway spills immediately. Switch to propylene glycol-based products where available.

### 3. Chocolate (toxic dose only)

The most over-reported "emergency" in dogs. **Sub-toxic doses do not require ER visits.** Toxic dose depends on dog weight and chocolate type:

- **Milk chocolate:** roughly > 1 oz per 10 lb body weight → start to be concerned
- **Dark chocolate:** roughly > 0.3 oz per 10 lb body weight
- **Baker's chocolate:** much lower threshold

**What to do:** Use a chocolate-toxicity calculator (search "VCA chocolate calculator"). Below threshold + no symptoms = monitor at home. Above threshold OR any neurological symptoms (tremors, seizures, severe agitation) = ER NOW.

### 4. Snake bite (venomous species)

**Symptoms:** Sudden swelling, puncture marks, pain, bleeding that won't clot, drooling. Symptoms onset within 30–60 minutes of bite.

**What to do:** ER NOW. Do not apply a tourniquet. Do not cut and suck. Keep the dog as still and as low-stress as possible. If you saw the snake, photograph it (from distance) to help antivenom selection. In the US, rattlesnake bites are the most common; coral snake and copperhead bites are species-specific.

**Prevention:** Rattlesnake vaccine in endemic areas is debated but real. On-leash hiking in known snake habitat. Avoidance training (some trainers offer this).

### 5. Heat stroke

**Who:** Brachycephalic breeds (Bulldogs, Pugs, Boxers, Boston Terriers, Frenchies) at highest risk; any dog left in a car or exercised in heat. Mortality is high — published case series put case-fatality at 30–50% once severe heat stroke is established.

**Symptoms:** Heavy panting that doesn't stop, brick-red gums, vomiting, collapse, seizures, body temp > 104.5 °F.

**What to do:** Cool with tepid (not ice-cold) water on the body, especially the belly, armpits, and paw pads. Fan moving air over wet fur. Drive to ER immediately while cooling. Stop cooling at 103.5 °F to avoid rebound hypothermia. Even if the dog looks recovered after cooling, the ER visit is still mandatory — internal damage (DIC, kidney injury) can develop hours later.

## Cats — top 5

### 1. Urethral obstruction (male cats)

**The single most time-critical feline emergency.** Crystals or a mucus plug block the male cat's narrow urethra. Bladder cannot empty. Within 24 hours, untreated, the cat develops life-threatening hyperkalemia (high potassium) that stops the heart.

**Symptoms:** Repeated trips to the litter box producing little or no urine. Crying or straining in the box. Hiding, lethargy, vomiting. A firm, painful belly when gently palpated.

**What to do:** ER NOW. This is one of the few emergencies that is fatal inside 24 hours. The ER will unblock under anaesthesia and stabilize potassium. With prompt treatment, survival is excellent. Without it, survival is near zero.

### 2. Lily ingestion (cats only)

True lilies (Lilium and Hemerocallis species — Easter lily, Stargazer, daylily) cause acute kidney failure in cats. Even tiny exposure — chewing a leaf, licking pollen off the fur, drinking water from a vase — can be fatal.

**Symptoms:** Vomiting within 2 hours, then a false-recovery period, then kidney failure within 24–72 hours.

**What to do:** ER NOW if you suspect any exposure. Even no symptoms. The window to start IV fluid protection is the first 6–18 hours; beyond that, the kidneys are often unrecoverable. Call Pet Poison Helpline en route.

### 3. Linear foreign body (string, ribbon, tinsel)

Cats love string. Swallowed string can saw through the intestinal wall as peristalsis tries to push it through. **Never pull on a string sticking out of a cat's mouth, throat, or anus** — you risk slicing the intestines from the inside.

**Symptoms:** Vomiting, abdominal pain, refusing to eat. Sometimes a visible thread coming out of one end.

**What to do:** ER NOW. Surgical removal is usually required.

**Prevention:** No string, ribbon, tinsel, hair-tie, or sewing thread accessible to cats. Sewing kits, especially, are a leading exposure source.

### 4. Toxic exposure to human medications

The top cat-toxic human medications: **acetaminophen (Tylenol) is severely toxic — one tablet can kill a cat.** Ibuprofen is dangerous. Many antidepressants (SSRIs, venlafaxine) attract cats and are highly toxic.

**Symptoms:** Highly variable — drooling, vomiting, brown gums (acetaminophen), tremors, hyperthermia, breathing difficulty.

**What to do:** ER NOW or Pet Poison Helpline first. Bring the pill bottle. Time is everything.

### 5. Respiratory distress (open-mouth breathing)

Already covered in email #3 — **open-mouth breathing in a cat is always abnormal.** Causes range from asthma to heart failure to pleural effusion (fluid around the lungs). All of them are ER-NOW.

**Symptoms:** Panting, sitting with elbows out, neck extended, breathing with the abdomen. Blue or grey gums.

**What to do:** ER NOW. Transport calmly — stress makes feline respiratory distress dramatically worse. Carry the cat in a familiar carrier with a towel partially draped over it.

## The pattern across all 10

Each of these emergencies has a **specific recognition cue** — bloat = unproductive retching in a large dog; antifreeze = false recovery; urethral obstruction = repeated litter-box trips with no urine; lily = cat near lilies; string = string in mouth or anus; open-mouth = cat panting.

If you can list those ten cues by heart, you'll catch ~80% of the truly time-critical emergencies — early.
