A pet first-aid kit is the cheapest emergency-prep investment you'll ever make. $40–$70 of supplies. One Saturday afternoon. Most of the contents stay relevant for 12 months.

Here is the list — and, more importantly, the reasoning for each item.

## The standard kit

### Diagnostic tools

**Digital rectal thermometer + petroleum jelly.** A pet thermometer reads in 10 seconds. Knowing your animal's temperature is the single fastest way to decide ER vs. monitor. Heat stroke (> 104.5 °F) is one of the most common preventable summer emergencies; hypothermia (< 99 °F) in small or wet animals goes the other direction. Keep the petroleum jelly with it.

**Capillary refill time check** — no equipment needed, but worth practicing. Press a fingertip against your pet's gum for one second, release, count seconds until the pale spot pinks back up. < 2 seconds is normal. > 3 seconds is a sign of shock or poor perfusion. Drilling this on your healthy pet now means you'll do it correctly when it matters.

### Bandaging

**Gauze pads** (2"×2" and 4"×4") and a roll of **self-adhesive cohesive bandage** (Vetrap or generic). Cohesive bandage sticks to itself, not to fur, so you can wrap a paw or leg without taping the animal.

**Blunt-tip bandage scissors.** Sharp scissors cut bandage; blunt tip means you won't stab a panicked animal. EMT shears work well.

### Wound care

**Chlorhexidine antiseptic wipes.** Use these to clean small wounds. Do NOT use hydrogen peroxide on wounds — it damages tissue and slows healing, despite generations of well-meaning advice to the contrary. The AVMA and most modern wound-care references both note this.

**Saline eye-rinse** (preservative-free, sold for human use). Flushing a foreign body or chemical out of an eye is time-sensitive; tap water works in an emergency but saline is gentler.

**Styptic powder** (Kwik Stop or similar). For nail bleeds, which look terrifying and stop in under a minute with pressure plus styptic. The single most common false-alarm ER cause.

### Toxin response — with caveats

**3% hydrogen peroxide.** ONLY to induce vomiting in dogs, and ONLY when explicitly instructed by a vet or one of the poison-control hotlines. NEVER in cats — it causes severe stomach ulceration in feline GI tracts. Call the hotline first, every time.

**ASPCA Animal Poison Control:** 888-426-4435 (fee applies)
**Pet Poison Helpline:** 855-764-7661 (fee applies)

Both lines are 24/7 and dramatically cheaper than the wrong ER decision. Print these on a sticky note and attach it to your kit.

### Restraint & transport

**A muzzle sized for your dog.** An injured dog will bite — even a dog who has never bitten in his life, even the gentlest dog you've ever met. A basket muzzle that allows panting is the safest choice. (Brachycephalic breeds need a specific shape.)

**A clean towel** large enough to use as a stretcher for a small-to-medium pet. Two people, one at each end, can carry an injured animal to the car without bending its spine.

**Nitrile gloves.** Blood, vomit, and unknown substances are not pleasant. Gloves cost almost nothing.

### Paperwork

**A printed pet medical record + current medication list.** When you arrive at an ER you've never been to, the staff cannot pull your pet's records from your primary vet at 2 a.m. A printout in the kit shaves 30 minutes off intake.

**Phone numbers card.** Primary vet, nearest 24-hour ER, telehealth backup (Vetster, AskVet, or Pawp), the two poison hotlines above.

## Species add-ons

- **Dog:** Plain diphenhydramine (Benadryl) — for mild allergic reactions or bee stings. Confirm your dog's dose with your vet and **write it on the kit label** so you're not converting milligrams at 3 a.m.
- **Cat:** A pillowcase or large towel for safe burrito-restraint. Pediatric oral syringes (no needle) for administering oral medication.
- **Ferret:** Karo syrup or honey — for suspected hypoglycemia, which is common in ferrets with insulinoma. A small dab on the gums while you drive to the ER can buy you time.
- **Rabbit:** Simethicone infant gas drops — first-line for early GI stasis when paired with a vet call. A critical-care feeding syringe is worth having, but only use it under vet direction.
- **Senior or chronically ill pet:** A current medication list with doses and last-dose times, plus a copy of the most recent bloodwork. Saves enormous time on intake.

## Where to buy

Most components are at any pharmacy (CVS, Walgreens). A few specialty items — pet-specific muzzle, styptic powder, Vetrap — are easier to source online. We're hesitant to recommend pre-built first-aid kits because they almost always include filler items (bandage sizes you'll never use, a "thermometer probe cover" that doesn't fit a standard digital thermometer) and skip items you actually need.

A reasonable shopping list to add to a Chewy or Amazon cart for the dog/cat items: thermometer, Vetrap, gauze, chlorhexidine wipes, styptic powder, muzzle, towel. Total roughly $40–$60. Chlorhexidine and saline at the pharmacy. Print the records yourself.

## What to skip

- **Pre-built "deluxe" kits** with 47 items. Most of them aren't used and you'll forget which sub-pouch the thermometer is in.
- **Antibiotic ointments** (Neosporin and equivalents) on cats — they will lick it off and ingest the petroleum base; many also contain neomycin, which carries risk. Use chlorhexidine instead.
- **Aspirin and ibuprofen** — neither belongs in a pet kit. Ibuprofen is dangerous in dogs and severely toxic in cats. Aspirin has narrow margins. If your pet needs an anti-inflammatory, it's a vet decision, not a kit decision.
