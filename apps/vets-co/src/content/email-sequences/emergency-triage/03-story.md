A reader, Maria, sent us a short note after a 2 a.m. episode with her elderly cat. With her permission, lightly edited:

> "Cleo woke us at 2 a.m. yowling and hiding under the bed. She came out when I called. I checked her — alert, gum colour normal, no distended abdomen, no laboured breathing, no obvious pain when I palpated. She'd vomited a small amount of clear fluid by the cabinet. I took her temperature; 101.8 °F. Heart rate 180.
>
> The card put her in the yellow band, not the red. I left a voicemail for the clinic and went back to bed with her on my chest. I checked her every two hours until morning.
>
> By 8 a.m. she had eaten a third of her breakfast and used the litter box normally. I called the clinic; they had us in by 11. The diagnosis was a mild upper respiratory flare-up — exactly what the day-shift vet had been monitoring her for over the previous month.
>
> Without the card I would have driven to the ER at 2 a.m. The ER visit would have been $800 to confirm what we already knew. I would not have slept. Cleo would have hated me for the next two days.
>
> The card didn't replace the vet. It told me which vet, and when."

Two points worth pulling out of Maria's note:

1. **The card is calibrated against the AAHA emergency triage criteria.** It is not a substitute for clinical judgement; it is a tool to help an owner reach the *right* clinical judgement faster.

2. **Maria checked vital signs before she made a decision.** That single habit — checking before calling — is the most consequential thing the card teaches.
