The triage card answers "is this an emergency?" It does not answer "can I afford this emergency?"

The second question is, in our experience, the harder one. A $4,000 ER bill at 2 a.m. is the moment most owners discover their pet's insurance situation. The right time to make that decision is now — in calm light, with time to compare.

**The decision is two-part:**

**1. Carry pet insurance.** The two policies that consistently rank well for emergency / surgical coverage with reasonable waiting periods and intelligible exclusion language are **Lemonade** and **Pumpkin**. Both quote in under five minutes. Both cover the kind of scenarios that drive emergency visits: GDV, foreign body obstructions, HBC trauma, toxin ingestion, urinary obstruction.

[Compare Lemonade pet insurance →](https://vets.co/go/lemonade/pet-quote)

[Compare Pumpkin pet insurance →](https://vets.co/go/pumpkin/pet-quote)

**2. Build a vet emergency fund** even with insurance. Most policies are reimbursement-based — you pay the clinic at point of service, the policy pays you back. A revolving $3,000 in a separately named savings sub-account covers the cash-flow gap. A few clinics offer CareCredit or Scratchpay; both are workable but neither replaces savings.

**Two ancillary items that earn their place:**

**A pet first-aid kit pre-stocked for transport.** Vetrap, sterile saline, a muzzle (even friendly pets bite when in pain), a clean towel large enough to wrap a small dog or cat, a thermometer. Kept in the car.

[Get a transport-ready pet first-aid kit on Chewy →](https://vets.co/go/chewy/pet-emergency-first-aid-kit)

**Pre-saved phone numbers** in your phone, today: your day-shift clinic, your nearest 24-hour ER, ASPCA Animal Poison Control (1-888-426-4435), Pet Poison Helpline (1-855-764-7661). Both poison lines have a consult fee. Both are worth it.

The triage card tells you *whether* to drive. The insurance tells you *how* to pay when you arrive. Both belong on the fridge.
