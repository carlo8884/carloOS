The most useful skill in a real emergency isn't first aid — it's being able to tell the ER vet, in the first 30 seconds of the call, "her heart rate is 180, respiratory rate 50, gums are pale, capillary refill is 4 seconds."

That single sentence changes the triage in a way that "she seems off" can't.

Here is how to take each one, by species. Practice on your healthy pet now. Three minutes a week. The technique is identical when it matters; the only difference is you'll be calm.

## Heart rate

### Dogs

Place your fingers (not thumb — your thumb has its own pulse) on the femoral artery, which runs along the inside of the back leg, high up where the leg meets the body. You can feel it as a soft groove. Count beats for 15 seconds, multiply by 4.

Alternative: place your palm flat against the chest, just behind the elbow on the left side, and count thumps.

**Adult dog normal:** 70–120 bpm (large breeds tend low; small breeds run higher; puppies under 6 months can hit 180 normally).

### Cats

Same femoral artery location, but cats are smaller — most owners get a better reading by pressing a palm against the chest just behind the left front leg.

**Adult cat normal:** 140–220 bpm. Cats in clinic settings often read high due to stress; the home reading is the truer baseline. This is why the home measurement matters.

### Ferrets

Press a fingertip gently against the chest just behind the front legs. Ferret hearts are tiny and fast — you'll feel a flutter more than distinct beats.

**Ferret normal:** 180–250 bpm.

### Rabbits

Same chest method. Rabbits have very fast heart rates and are easily stressed by restraint — keep the measurement short.

**Rabbit normal:** 130–325 bpm (wide range; baseline is what matters).

## Respiratory rate

The simplest vital. Watch the chest or flank rise and fall. One inhale + one exhale = one breath. Count for 30 seconds, multiply by 2.

Take this measurement when your pet is **sleeping or fully at rest** — not panting, not just back from a walk, not after stress.

- **Dog:** 15–30 breaths/min at rest
- **Cat:** 20–30 breaths/min at rest. Sleeping respiratory rate > 30 is the single most reliable home red flag for heart failure — if your cat has a heart murmur or is on heart medication, your vet may ask you to track this weekly.
- **Ferret:** 33–36 breaths/min
- **Rabbit:** 32–60 breaths/min

### One absolute rule for cats

**Open-mouth breathing in a cat is ALWAYS abnormal.**

Cats are obligate nasal breathers when healthy. A cat panting like a dog is in respiratory distress — drive to the ER, do not wait, do not call first. This is the single most important cat-specific data point on the entire triage card.

## Temperature

Yes, it's rectal. There is no reliable non-contact pet thermometer for home use. A standard digital human thermometer works fine. Lubricate with petroleum jelly. Insert about 1 inch (less for cats and small animals). Reading takes 10–30 seconds.

- **Dog:** 101.0–102.5 °F (38.3–39.2 °C)
- **Cat:** 100.5–102.5 °F (38.1–39.2 °C)
- **Ferret:** 100.0–104.0 °F (37.8–40.0 °C) — wide normal range
- **Rabbit:** 101.3–104.0 °F (38.5–40.0 °C)

**Hyperthermia red lines:**
- Dog or cat > 103.5 °F: call within hours
- Dog or cat > 104.5 °F: heat-stroke territory, ER NOW
- Dog or cat > 106 °F: organ damage begins; immediate emergency

**Hypothermia red lines:**
- < 99 °F in any small mammal: warm gently, call vet. Common in soaked or post-anaesthesia pets.
- < 96 °F: severe; emergency

## Capillary refill time (CRT)

Lift your pet's lip. Press your fingertip against a pink section of gum for one second. Release. Count seconds until the pale spot pinks back up.

- **Normal:** < 2 seconds
- **Slow (> 3 seconds):** dehydration, shock, or poor perfusion — call your vet
- **Very fast (< 1 second):** sometimes seen with sepsis or early shock, less commonly meaningful

## Gum colour

While you're already looking at the gums for CRT, note the colour:

- **Pink and moist:** normal
- **Pale or white:** anaemia, shock, severe blood loss — ER NOW
- **Brick red:** heat stroke, sepsis — ER NOW
- **Blue or grey:** oxygen deprivation — ER NOW, do not delay
- **Yellow:** jaundice (liver disease) — same-day vet
- **Brown:** rodenticide poisoning (warfarin types) or severe acetaminophen toxicity in cats — ER NOW

## Why build a baseline now

Your pet's "normal" is your pet's normal — not the textbook average. A 95-bpm resting heart rate is fine for one Labrador and a sign of trouble for another. Knowing the resting heart rate for **your animal** lets you spot a change.

Take and write down (or note in your phone) these numbers once a month on a healthy day:

- Resting heart rate
- Sleeping respiratory rate
- Normal gum colour

Three minutes a month. The first time it actually matters, you'll have a comparison point — and the vet on the phone will instantly take you more seriously.

## This week's homework

Pick a quiet evening. Cat asleep, dog flopped on the rug. Take all five vitals. Write them down. Stick the note on the back of the fridge card.

Do it again in 30 days, on a different healthy day. That's your baseline.
