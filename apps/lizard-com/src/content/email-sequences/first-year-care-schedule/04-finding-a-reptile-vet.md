Find a reptile-experienced vet now, in the routine-wellness window, not later, in the 11 PM emergency window. This is the single most important non-husbandry decision in the first year.

Here is the practical version.

**The default problem.** Most general-practice small-animal vets see dogs and cats. A subset see "exotics" — which means rabbits, ferrets, guinea pigs, and birds, plus the occasional reptile. A smaller subset see reptiles frequently enough to be confident with the husbandry-disease pattern recognition that reptiles require. The standard of care across those three tiers is meaningfully different.

For an animal whose first-year mortality is largely driven by husbandry-related disease — metabolic bone disease, respiratory infection, parasites, thermal burns — the difference between "any vet" and "a reptile vet" matters more than for most companion species.

**The ARAV directory.** The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians publishes a member directory at arav.org. ARAV members have, at a minimum, signaled professional interest in reptile and amphibian medicine; many are board-certified or have completed reptile-focused residencies. The directory is searchable by region. Start there.

**Questions to ask before the first appointment.**

1. "How many reptile patients does the clinic see per week?" Looking for a number, not "we see reptiles". Once a month is light; multiple per week is meaningful.
2. "Do you do in-house fecal floats and microscopy?" Most husbandry disease starts with parasites. In-house fecals are faster and cheaper than send-out.
3. "Do you do reptile bloodwork in-house, and what reference ranges do you use?" Reptile reference ranges differ by species; a generic mammalian panel applied to a reptile mis-reads.
4. "What is the cost of a standard new-patient wellness exam?" Typical exotic wellness exam fees in the US are roughly $80–$180 depending on region (not including bloodwork or diagnostics). If a clinic won't quote, that itself is informative.
5. "Do you do after-hours emergency, or do you have a relationship with an emergency exotic referral?" Important to know in advance, not at 11 PM.

**What to bring to the first visit.**

- The animal in a small ventilated container (a small Sterilite or pillowcase for snakes), warm and dark.
- A fecal sample if possible (refrigerated, less than 24 hours old).
- Photos of the enclosure setup — top-down, side-view, basking spot.
- Your equipment list: UVB bulb model and install date, thermostat model, thermometer model, supplement products.
- The current schedule (the one you printed from this email series).

The husbandry review is usually as valuable as the physical exam. A first-year animal whose husbandry is good rarely has anything else wrong.

**Cost framework.** A baseline first-year vet budget, US:

- New-patient wellness exam + fecal float: ~$120–$220
- Annual recheck at month 12: ~$80–$180
- Treatment for a common first-year issue (parasite course, mild RI): ~$150–$400 on top of exam
- Emergency exotic visit: $250–$600 minimum; a difficult diagnosis can climb significantly higher

Pet insurance for reptiles exists but is limited. Set aside the budget instead.

**When to ER, when to wait for primary.** Active bleeding, prolapse, respiratory distress (gaping with mucus, audible wheezing), seizures or tetanic tremors, full-body limp paralysis, severe burns — ER, immediately. Off-feed for a week, dull skin, mild irregular shed, a single missed defecation — primary, next routine slot.
