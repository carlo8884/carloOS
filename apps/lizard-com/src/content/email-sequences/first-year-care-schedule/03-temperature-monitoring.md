Reptiles are ectotherms. Their immune function, digestion, and behaviour all depend on operating in a specific thermal range. Outside that range — even by a few degrees, for a sustained period — disease risk climbs.

The first-year failure is not "I set it wrong on day one." The first-year failure is **thermal drift**: a slow degree-per-month shift, invisible without a log, that puts the animal outside its operational range by month nine. Here is how to prevent it.

**Gradient, not target.** A reptile enclosure is a gradient. The animal thermoregulates by moving between zones. A "78°F enclosure" is meaningless; you want a basking spot at the species-specific high end, a cool side at the species-specific low end, and a continuous temperature gradient between them. Typical starter targets (always verify against species references):

- **Leopard gecko**: basking 88–92°F surface temp; cool side 75–80°F; ambient 75°F.
- **Bearded dragon**: basking 95–110°F surface temp (juveniles higher); cool side 75–85°F; ambient 80°F.
- **Ball python**: basking 88–92°F; cool side 75–80°F; ambient 78–80°F.
- **Crested gecko**: room temperature, 72–78°F ambient. No basking spot needed. Above ~82°F sustained is dangerous.
- **Corn snake**: basking 85°F; cool side 70–75°F.

**Measure where the animal actually is.** Use an infrared temperature gun pointed at the floor or basking surface — not at the animal's eyes (laser pointers in eyes are not great), and not at the air. Surface temperature is what matters; ambient air temperature is secondary. A digital thermometer with a probe at the basking surface is the other standard.

**The daily-log habit.** Once a day, at the same time, record:

1. Basking surface temperature
2. Cool-side air temperature
3. Ambient humidity

Two minutes. A small notebook by the enclosure works. The reason this matters: drift is invisible from one day to the next, but a column of 30 numbers reveals it immediately. A basking spot reading 92°F on day 1 and 86°F on day 30, with no single noticeable change, is a thermostat or bulb degrading. You catch it on the log.

**The "thermostat alone" trap.** A thermostat regulates the heating element to a target set at the probe location. It does not measure the basking surface, and it does not tell you when the heat lamp's reflector has dust on it, when the bulb has aged, or when room ambient has shifted (winter, AC, drafts). A thermostat reduces drift; it does not eliminate it. Spot-check the basking surface weekly.

**Common drift causes.** Seasonal room temperature change. Reflector dust on a heat bulb. Heat panel ageing. Probe drift on cheap thermostats. Enclosure cover changes (mesh dirty, glass cleaned, substrate depth changed). All scheduleable.

**The minimum equipment.** A reliable thermostat (proportional or pulse-proportional), a separate digital thermometer with a probe, and an infrared spot thermometer. Sticker-style adhesive thermometers are inadequate for primary measurement — they have a wide tolerance and stick where they happen to be, not where the animal is.
