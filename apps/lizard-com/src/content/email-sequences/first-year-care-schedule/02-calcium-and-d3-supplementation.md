Most insect feeders have an inverted calcium-to-phosphorus ratio. They are not, by themselves, calcium-adequate food. This is one of the central reasons reptile husbandry includes calcium dusting at all.

Here is the version of this story that fits on a fridge magnet.

**The Ca:P ratio.** Reptile bone health depends on a dietary Ca:P ratio of roughly 2:1. Looking at unsupplemented feeders (Mader, *Reptile Medicine and Surgery*, 3rd ed., nutrition chapter):

- Crickets: ~1:9 Ca:P (badly inverted)
- Mealworms: ~1:14 Ca:P (the worst commonly-fed insect; mealworm-only diets fail)
- Dubia roaches: ~1:3 (better than crickets, still inverted)
- Black soldier fly larvae (BSFL): close to 1.5:1 (one of the few naturally calcium-positive insects)
- Pinkie mice: ~1:1 (juvenile bone is undeveloped — pinkies are not a "calcium source")
- Adult mice and rats: closer to 1:2 (the calcium comes from the bones)

Crickets and mealworms are calorically efficient but they will not feed a juvenile reptile to skeletal adequacy unsupplemented. That is what dusting is for.

**Ca-no-D3 vs Ca-with-D3.** Two distinct products. Use them differently.

- **Calcium without D3** (Rep-Cal Phosphorus-Free Calcium, Repashy SuperCal NoD): the default dust if the animal has adequate UVB. The animal makes its own D3 from sun-on-skin physics.
- **Calcium with D3** (Rep-Cal with D3, Repashy SuperCal LoD): the substitute for UVB. Used either when there is no UVB at all, or to supplement an under-UVB enclosure. Over-dosing D3 is a real risk — D3 is fat-soluble; calcium itself is not.

**Schedule starting point.** Refined by your vet for your species:

- **Juvenile insectivores** (leopard gecko, juvenile beardie): Ca-no-D3 every feeding; Ca+D3 2–3× per week if UVB is marginal.
- **Adult insectivores**: Ca-no-D3 2–3× per week; Ca+D3 1× per week if UVB is adequate.
- **Herbivores** (uromastyx, sulcata tortoise, adult beardie greens): Ca-no-D3 dusted on greens 2–3× per week.
- **Carnivores eating whole prey** (ball python, corn snake, boa): no dusting; the prey provides Ca via bone. Confirm with vet.

**Gutloading still matters.** Even with dusting, what the feeder ate 24–48 hours before you fed it to the reptile changes its nutritional content. We'll cover gutloading in a later email.

**The failure mode this prevents.** Nutritional metabolic bone disease (NMBD) — the most common reptile husbandry disease, and one of the most preventable. It is mechanically a calcium deficit (from inadequate intake, inadequate D3, or both). The acute end of the calcium-deficit spectrum is hypocalcemia — tetanic muscle contractions and seizures. The chronic end is bone deformity. Both are addressed by the dust schedule above.
