Gutloading is the underused half of feeder nutrition. The dust you put on the cricket addresses calcium. What the cricket ate 24–48 hours before you fed it to the reptile addresses everything else.

**What gutloading is.** A live feeder insect is a delivery vehicle. The macros, micros, and water it carries are largely determined by what it consumed in the 24–48 hours immediately before you fed it to the reptile. A cricket fed dry pet food and a wedge of carrot in the pet-store cup is a calorically thin, low-water, low-micronutrient feeder. The same cricket fed a quality commercial gutload plus fresh greens for two days is a different food.

This is well-documented (Allen & Oftedal, *Zoo Biology*; multiple reptile-nutrition references in Mader, *Reptile Medicine and Surgery*).

**Commercial gutloads.** A handful of products are formulated specifically as gutloads — not feeder food generally, but feed designed to load nutrients into the insect within 24–48 hours of consumption:

- **Repashy Bug Burger** — a moist, palatable gutload; widely used.
- **Repashy SuperLoad** — drier, longer-shelf-life formula.
- **Mazuri Better Bug** — a commercial gutload from a serious nutrition manufacturer.
- **Arcadia InsectFuel** — a more recent UK-origin product, available in the US.

These are not "cricket food" in the sense of keeping the cricket alive long-term; they are nutrition-dense diets used in the 24–48 hours before feeding to the reptile.

**DIY gutloading.** Fresh produce works, with the proviso that some plants are poor sources of usable nutrients:

- **Good**: dark leafy greens (collards, mustard greens, dandelion), squash, sweet potato, papaya, carrot.
- **Avoid as gutload bulk**: lettuce (mostly water, low nutrients), spinach (oxalates bind calcium), broccoli (goitrogenic in excess), kale in excess.

Combine with a small dish of commercial dry food (Mazuri, Repashy) and a moisture source.

**Why mealworm-only diets fail.** Mealworms are convenient — they keep in the fridge, they don't chirp, they don't escape. They also have a Ca:P ratio around 1:14, a chitinous exoskeleton that is poorly digested, and very low water content. A juvenile leopard gecko fed mealworms exclusively is a candidate for both impaction and metabolic bone disease. Mealworms as one component of a varied diet are fine; mealworms as the sole feeder are not.

**Prey-size rule.** Standard reptile-husbandry guidance: a prey item should be no larger than the distance between the animal's eyes. For snakes, the rule is a girth-based one — prey roughly the same girth as the widest part of the snake (not the head, the widest part of the body). Adult mice for an adult corn snake; pinkies for a hatchling. Over-sized prey is a regurgitation risk; under-sized prey requires more frequent feedings.

**Frequency starting points.** Refined by species and life stage. Juveniles eat more frequently than adults. Carnivores eat less frequently than insectivores. The schedule's species table gives a starting cadence:

- Leopard gecko juvenile: small crickets / dubia, dusted, 5–6× per week
- Leopard gecko adult: dusted feeders, 2–3× per week
- Bearded dragon juvenile: insects 1–2× daily plus daily greens; insect-to-plant ratio ~70:30
- Bearded dragon adult: insects 2–3× per week; greens daily; ratio inverts to ~30:70
- Ball python: one appropriately-sized rodent every 7–14 days, less often for adults
- Corn snake: one rodent every 5–10 days for juveniles; every 10–14 days for adults
- Crested gecko: commercial powdered diet (Repashy, Pangea) 3–4× per week; insects 1× per week as enrichment
