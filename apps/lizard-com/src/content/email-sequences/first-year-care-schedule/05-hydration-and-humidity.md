Hydration is two coupled problems: drinking water, and ambient humidity. Both have species-specific targets, and both have well-known first-year failures.

Here is the version that fits on the schedule.

**Species-specific humidity ranges.** Reptile species evolved across an enormous range of environments. There is no single "good humidity":

- **Leopard gecko**: 30–40% ambient; a humid hide at 70–80% for shedding. Sustained high humidity causes respiratory infection in this desert species.
- **Bearded dragon**: 30–40% ambient; below 20% sustained risks dehydration; above ~60% sustained risks respiratory issues.
- **Ball python**: 50–60% baseline; 70%+ during shed cycles. Below 40% sustained causes incomplete shed (dysecdysis).
- **Crested gecko**: 60–80% range; let it dry to ~50% between mistings rather than holding constant at 80% (mold and respiratory risk).
- **Corn snake**: 40–60% ambient; a small humid hide for shed.

The schedule's species column gives ranges for each common starter species. The ranges are not narrow targets — reptiles tolerate the swing well; what causes problems is sustained high (mold, respiratory) or sustained low (dysecdysis, dehydration).

**The "soaking schedule" trap.** A common piece of pet-store advice is "soak the animal weekly". For tropical species, this is fine; for desert species, it is actively harmful. Forced soaking of a leopard gecko or beardie at length, especially in deeper water, is a stress event and a respiratory risk. The water bowl is for self-selected drinking. Soaking is reserved for specific problems (dehydration confirmed by skin tent, retained shed on extremities, post-egg-laying recovery) — not a default weekly routine.

**Water bowl vs misting vs fogger.** Three different tools for three different problems:

- **Water bowl**: drinking water and a small humidity buffer. Refilled daily. Sized so the animal can drink but not submerge (small species) or so the animal can soak by choice (some pythons). Cleaned weekly.
- **Misting**: short-duration humidity spikes plus drinking surface for species that lick droplets (crested geckos, day geckos, chameleons). Once or twice daily depending on species and ambient.
- **Fogger / ultrasonic humidifier**: sustained ambient humidity in larger enclosures or arid rooms. The risk: a fogger pointed at the basking spot is a respiratory bomb (cold wet air on a warm reptile). Plumb it to the cool side.

**Ambient is what matters.** A hygrometer near the basking spot reads low because warm air holds less relative moisture per °F. A hygrometer in the cool zone, off the substrate, gives the more useful reading. Place the hygrometer where the animal spends time, not where it looks tidy.

**The hydration tell.** Skin tent is the field test: pinch a small fold of skin on the side of the body; in a hydrated reptile it returns instantly; in a dehydrated animal it stays tented. Sunken eyes, wrinkled skin, slow eye-cap shed, sticky mouth — all consistent with dehydration. Mild dehydration is corrected with environmental adjustment plus offered (not forced) soaking; significant dehydration is a vet visit.

**Why this matters in the first year.** A reptile that is chronically slightly under-hydrated rarely looks unwell — it just sheds incompletely, doesn't gain weight as fast, and is more vulnerable to stress events. The compound effect over twelve months is real. The schedule's daily humidity log catches the trend before the animal does.
