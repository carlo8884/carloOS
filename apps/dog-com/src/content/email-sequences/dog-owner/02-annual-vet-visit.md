The annual physical is the workhorse of preventive medicine in dogs. AAHA's (American Animal Hospital Association) standard recommends one comprehensive exam per year for healthy adult dogs, every 6 months for puppies (<1 year) and seniors (>7 years for most breeds, >5 years for giant breeds). It's also the appointment most owners under-use because they don't know what to ask.

---

**What the vet covers without prompting**

A standard adult annual visit typically covers:

- **Physical exam.** Eyes, ears, nose, mouth, teeth, lymph nodes, abdominal palpation, joint range of motion, skin and coat, body condition score.
- **Weight and body condition.** Tracked over time; a 10% weight change is meaningful in either direction.
- **Heart and lungs.** Auscultation; murmurs and arrhythmias are commonly first detected here.
- **Vaccination review.** Core (DHPP, rabies) and risk-based (Bordetella, leptospirosis, Lyme, influenza) per AAHA-approved schedules.
- **Heartworm test.** Annual antigen blood test; prevention is year-round.
- **Fecal exam.** Intestinal parasite screen.

---

**What to add to the conversation (the high-leverage questions)**

The annual exam is also when small concerns become detectable problems. Bring up:

- **Any change in drinking, urinating, eating, or weight in the last 6 months.** These are the four highest-yield home observations for early disease detection.
- **Any change in activity or mood.** Reluctance to walk up stairs, slowing down on walks, less interest in play. Often the first sign of pain.
- **Lumps or bumps.** Note size, location, growth rate. Most lumps in dogs are benign; the cheap insurance is having every new one assessed.
- **Dental status.** Periodontal disease is the most common chronic condition in adult dogs. Ask about home care, schedule a dental cleaning if recommended.
- **The senior dog conversation, starting at age 5–7.** Bloodwork baseline, joint health, cognitive change, what to monitor going into the next stage of life.

---

**The bloodwork question**

Annual screening bloodwork (CBC, chemistry panel, urinalysis) is an AAHA-recommended addition for adults from middle age (around age 5–7 in most breeds, earlier in large and giant breeds). It is the cheapest way to catch kidney disease, liver disease, endocrine disorders, and many other conditions before they are clinically obvious.

A baseline bloodwork at the first adult visit also gives the vet a "normal for this dog" reference point to compare against any future abnormal panel — particularly useful for the long-term picture.

For senior dogs (>7 years; >5 in giant breeds), the AAHA standard moves to every 6 months for both exams and bloodwork. The cost of an additional panel per year is small relative to the diagnostic value at that life stage.

---

**The single most useful thing you can do before any vet appointment**

Write down what you want to ask. Bring it. Real vet appointments are 15–20 minutes; the brain blanks. A list ensures you leave with the answers to your actual questions, not just the answers to whichever symptom the vet's exam surfaced.
