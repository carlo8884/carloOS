Dogs are evolutionarily inclined to mask pain — a behaviour inherited from their wild ancestors where visible weakness was a fitness liability. Most dogs in chronic pain do not vocalise it. They live with it. Owner observation is what catches it, often years before a clinical sign brings the dog to the vet.

The signs that slip past the masking are the ones to learn.

---

**Behavioural changes that often signal pain**

- **Reluctance to go up or down stairs.** Often the first sign of orthopaedic pain (hip, knee, elbow, spine). Particularly notable if the dog used to do stairs comfortably.
- **Slowing on walks; shorter walks; refusing walks.** A dog who used to want longer walks and now turns home early is telling you something.
- **Reluctance to jump up onto furniture or into the car.** A dog who used to leap easily and now hesitates or asks to be lifted.
- **A new "preferred side"** when lying down — favouring one side suggests joint discomfort on the other.
- **Pacing or restlessness at night.** Particularly common with osteoarthritis; the dog can't settle into a comfortable sleeping position.
- **A change in temperament** — less tolerant of children, grumpy when handled, snaps at other dogs that approach. Pain shortens the fuse.
- **Excessive licking at a specific spot** — joints, paws, tail base. Self-soothing for localised discomfort.
- **Reduced appetite.** Less specific than the orthopaedic signs but often present; pain can mute interest in food.

---

**Acute pain signs that are harder to miss**

- Vocalisation when picked up, touched, or moving in a specific way.
- Limp or three-legged movement.
- Inability to stand or rise.
- Visible swelling, wound, or limb deformity.
- Posture changes — hunched back, tucked abdomen, head held low.
- Trembling, panting, or rapid shallow breathing without exertion.

Any of these warrant a same-day vet contact. The acute signs are often easier — they bring the dog to the vet because they bring the owner's attention to the dog. The chronic signs are the ones to learn because they often don't.

---

**Osteoarthritis — the most under-treated chronic pain in adult dogs**

Osteoarthritis is the most common cause of chronic pain in adult dogs, estimated at >20% prevalence in dogs over age 1 and >80% in dogs over age 8 (peer-reviewed epidemiology in *Veterinary Surgery* and the *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association*). Most affected dogs live with it untreated because the home signs are subtle and the owner adapts to them gradually.

The honest news: osteoarthritis pain in dogs is well-managed. Weight management is the single highest-leverage intervention (every pound of excess body weight maps to multiple pounds of joint loading). NSAIDs designed for dogs, EPA / DHA supplementation, monthly Adequan injections, the newer monoclonal antibody options — all are real tools. A frank conversation with the vet about pain assessment is the entry point.

If your dog has slowed down "with age" — that's often pain, not just age.
