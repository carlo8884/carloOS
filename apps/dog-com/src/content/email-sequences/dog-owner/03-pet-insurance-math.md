Pet insurance is a real product category that is genuinely confusing because two policies that look similar can pay out very differently on the same claim. The honest answer to "should I get pet insurance" is "do the math against your dog's risk profile."

---

**How pet insurance actually works**

US pet insurance is reimbursement, not direct-pay: you pay the vet, submit the claim, and receive a partial reimbursement (usually 70–90% of covered costs above a deductible). It is closer to property insurance than to human health insurance — large-event protection, not routine-care coverage.

Standard policy structure:

- **Deductible** ($100–$1,000 typical) — annual or per-condition.
- **Reimbursement percentage** (70%, 80%, 90% typical).
- **Annual coverage limit** ($5K, $10K, unlimited typical).
- **Waiting periods** (14 days for accidents, often 6 months for orthopaedic conditions, varies for cruciate).
- **Pre-existing condition exclusion** (universal across the industry — a condition diagnosed or symptomatic before coverage starts is excluded).

---

**The premium-vs-payout math, in real numbers**

Industry data and aggregated published carrier rate-cards show the typical economic picture:

- **Annual premium for a healthy adult mixed-breed:** $40–80/month ($480–960/year).
- **Annual premium for a high-claim breed (Bulldogs, French Bulldogs, large breeds with cruciate / hip / cardiac predispositions):** $80–150/month ($960–1,800/year).
- **Average lifetime claim payout** if used: published carrier data ranges $3,500–7,000 per insured dog over the policy lifetime.
- **Break-even point:** roughly 4–8 years of premium payments equal the average lifetime payout. For premium breeds and large breeds, the math is closer to break-even or favourable.

**When the math favours insurance:**

- **Large or giant breeds** with high orthopaedic / cardiac claim rates (Great Danes, Mastiffs, Bernese Mountain Dogs).
- **Brachycephalic breeds** (Bulldogs, French Bulldogs, Pugs, Boston Terriers) with high airway-surgery rates.
- **Breeds with known genetic risks** (Goldens with cancer, Cavaliers with mitral valve disease, Dachshunds with IVDD).
- **Young dogs** — the longer the runway, the more chance the policy pays out for a major event.

**When the math is closer to break-even:**

- **Mixed-breed dogs of unknown background** with no known predispositions.
- **Older dogs** at first insurance — premiums climb sharply with age, pre-existing conditions accumulate.

---

**The non-financial argument**

Even when the math is close to break-even, insurance changes the decision space at a vet appointment. A $4,000 cruciate repair on a four-year-old large-breed dog is a real financial decision for many households; with 80% insurance reimbursement it becomes a logistical one. Owners who carry insurance more often pursue the recommended treatment without weighing it against rent.

That's not a math argument; it's a quality-of-care argument. It's worth pricing too.

---

**What to compare across carriers**

- Per-condition vs annual deductible
- Coverage of hereditary / congenital / breed-specific conditions
- Waiting periods (the big ones: orthopaedic, cruciate)
- Per-incident or annual caps
- Claim-processing speed (varies materially across carriers)
- What's explicitly excluded (dental disease, behavioural conditions, alternative therapy)
