If you're following along, your puppy is around 16–18 weeks now. The schedule is working. The crate is fine. Sit is solid. Walks are calm.

Heads up: this will not last.

Somewhere between six and ten months your dog will appear to forget everything they've learned. The recall that was 95% reliable will drop to 40%. The "sit" they did automatically will require a treat. They'll start pulling on leash. They might bark at people they used to love. They might mount things. They might selectively ignore you in a way that feels personal.

It's not personal. It's adolescence — and it's biologically as predictable as the socialization window was. Surveys of shelter intake show adolescence (6–18 months) is the single peak surrender age — owners think the dog is "broken" or "deliberately defiant". The dog isn't broken. The frontal cortex is restructuring while sex hormones rise. The well-trained puppy is still in there.

**Three rules for the adolescent period:**

1. **Don't loosen the schedule.** Keep crate, feeding times, training sessions, exercise quotas. This is exactly when most owners drop structure ("they're older now"). This is exactly when structure matters most.
2. **Re-teach, don't punish.** When the dog "forgets" a command, treat it like new training: rewards, short sessions, brief reps. Punishment at this stage installs the fear-based reactivity you spent the puppy weeks preventing.
3. **Long leash for recall.** Until the dog has been reliable in distraction at 9 months, recall practice happens on a long line (15–30 ft check cord), not off-leash. One bad off-leash recall (running into traffic, chasing a deer) can damage recall reliability for years.

Spay/neuter timing intersects with adolescence and is breed-specific — larger breeds increasingly recommended to wait until 12–18 months after growth-plate closure. Talk to your vet about timing for your specific breed; do not follow the old default of "fix at 6 months" without that conversation.
