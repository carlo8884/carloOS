If you only buy one piece of equipment that isn't the tank, filter, or heater, it's a **liquid test kit**. Without numbers you're guessing, and guessing is what kills tanks.

**Liquid drop kits, not strips.** The standard reference kit in the hobby is the API Freshwater Master Test Kit. It uses titrated reagents and colour-match cards for ammonia (NH3/NH4), nitrite (NO2), nitrate (NO3), and pH. Strips are convenient but their precision in the critical 0–1 ppm ammonia and nitrite range is poor — at the exact concentrations that determine whether your fish live, strips often read "0" when the real value is 0.25 or 0.50 ppm. Drop kits resolve that range.

**The four reads that matter during a cycle:**

1. **Ammonia (NH3/NH4).** What you dose at the start of a fishless cycle. Target during cycle: 2–4 ppm. Target when cycled: 0.
2. **Nitrite (NO2).** Appears once ammonia-oxidising bacteria are established. Climbs sharply, then crashes. Target when cycled: 0.
3. **Nitrate (NO3).** The end-product. Appears once nitrite-oxidising bacteria are established. Target when cycled: measurable (5–40 ppm typical).
4. **pH.** Affects how much of the ammonia in your tank is in the toxic NH3 form vs. the relatively safe NH4+ form. Mostly stable in tap water — but track it because a crashing pH stalls the cycle.

**What TDS doesn't tell you.** Total Dissolved Solids meters are cheap and read fast, but TDS is a bulk number — it lumps every dissolved ion together. A TDS of 250 ppm could be mostly calcium and magnesium (fine) or mostly nitrate and phosphate (a sign of an overstocked, under-maintained tank). TDS is useful for tracking change over time. It is not a substitute for the four individual reads above.

**This week:** if you don't already have a drop kit, get one. Begin testing daily. Write the numbers down — a sheet on the side of the tank works. The shape of the curve matters more than any single reading.

— Fish.com Editorial
