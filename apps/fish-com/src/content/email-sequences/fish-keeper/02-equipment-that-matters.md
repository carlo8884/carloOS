The aquarium hobby sells a lot of equipment. Most of it is optional. Three categories — filtration, heating, and lighting — are not optional, and getting any of them wrong creates a tank that is permanently working against itself.

---

**Filtration — size the filter for the bioload, not the tank**

The filter does two things: mechanical (removing particulates) and biological (housing the nitrifying bacteria from the cycle). For a freshwater community tank, the working rule is filter flow of **4–6× tank volume per hour**, with media volume sized for the planned stocking density rather than the tank dimensions.

Filter types:

- **Hang-on-back (HOB)** — easiest to maintain, suitable for tanks up to ~55 gal with moderate stocking. AquaClear (now Fluval), Aqueon QuietFlow, Marineland Penguin are the standard options.
- **Canister** — higher capacity, more media flexibility, quieter, better for tanks 40+ gal and any tank with heavy bioload (cichlids, large fish, planted aquascapes). Fluval, Eheim, Oase are the categories' senior brands.
- **Sponge filter (air-driven)** — gentle, cheap, excellent for breeding tanks, fry tanks, shrimp tanks. Limited mechanical filtration; pair with a HOB on display tanks.
- **Sump (saltwater / reef)** — separate compartment below the tank housing filtration, skimmer, heater, dosing equipment. Standard for marine display tanks 40+ gal.

The most common filtration mistake is under-sizing — buying the filter that's rated for the tank's gallon size, then stocking it more heavily than the rating assumed. The fix is to size up at purchase, not retrofit later.

---

**Heating — temperature stability matters as much as temperature target**

Most tropical freshwater species do best at 76–80°F; most marine systems run 76–82°F. The heater's job is to keep that temperature within a narrow band (±1°F is achievable on quality heaters) regardless of room-temperature swings.

- **Submersible adjustable heaters** (Cobalt Neo-Therm, Eheim Jager, Fluval E) are the default for any tank 5+ gal. Glass-encased models can break if the tank is bumped or run dry; titanium-coil models are more durable.
- **In-line heaters** (Hydor inline) sit on a canister filter's return line — useful for aquascaped tanks where hardware in the display tank is undesirable.
- **Sizing rule of thumb:** 3–5 watts per gallon of tank water. Under-sizing means the heater runs constantly and wears out fast; over-sizing means temperature overshoot if the thermostat fails.

A second redundant heater (each sized at 50–60% of the calculated wattage) is the standard reef-keeping safety practice — protects against a single-heater failure cooking or freezing the tank. Cheap insurance on any expensive livestock.

---

**Lighting — match the light to the tank's biology**

Lighting decisions depend entirely on what's in the tank:

- **Fish-only freshwater** — almost any LED in the 6500K range works. Inexpensive ($30–80 for a standard 20–55 gal tank).
- **Planted freshwater** — light intensity (PAR at substrate) drives plant growth. Low-light plants: any modern LED. High-light plants and CO₂-injected tanks: Finnex Stingray, Fluval Plant 3.0, Twinstar, ADA models. Light too dim → plants die back; too intense without CO₂ → algae crisis.
- **Saltwater reef** — corals require specific light spectra (heavy blue, ~14000–20000K) and intensity (PAR varies by coral type). Kessil A360X, Radion XR15, AI Hydra, Noopsyche, ATI. Specific stocking decisions depend on the light.

The single biggest lighting mistake is over-lighting a tank without CO₂ in freshwater (algae crisis) or under-lighting a reef tank (coral bleaching / starvation).
