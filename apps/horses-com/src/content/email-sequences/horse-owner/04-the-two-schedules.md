Most preventive-medicine outcome in horses comes from running two schedules well: vaccinations and dental. Both are framework-driven, both are well-documented in the American Association of Equine Practitioners (AAEP) guidelines, and both are categories where the difference between "doing it right" and "doing it whenever I remember" produces measurably different long-run health.

---

**Vaccinations — the AAEP framework**

The AAEP separates equine vaccinations into **core** and **risk-based**.

**Core vaccines** are recommended for every horse, every year, regardless of region or use:

- **Eastern and Western equine encephalomyelitis (EEE/WEE)** — mosquito-borne; both are frequently fatal once symptomatic.
- **West Nile virus** — mosquito-borne; commonly fatal or causes lasting neurological damage.
- **Tetanus** — universal exposure risk from any wound.
- **Rabies** — universally fatal; required by many state laws.

**Risk-based vaccines** depend on use, travel, and regional disease burden:

- **Equine influenza** — for any horse that travels, shows, or boards in a high-traffic facility.
- **Equine herpesvirus (EHV-1/EHV-4)** — same population; outbreaks are well-documented in showing populations.
- **Strangles** — for any horse exposed to high-traffic populations or new arrivals.
- **Botulism, Potomac Horse Fever, Leptospirosis** — region-and-management-dependent; ask your vet.

The simplest version: core vaccines annually for every horse; risk-based vaccines per your vet's protocol for your specific situation. Don't skip a year on the core list — the cost of a single fatal case (EEE, WNV, tetanus, rabies) is so high relative to the annual vaccine cost that the calculation isn't close.

**Dental — the underused half of preventive medicine**

Horse teeth grow continuously (hypsodont dentition) and wear unevenly. Sharp enamel points, hooks, ramps, and waves develop predictably and cause pain, weight loss, bit resistance, and quidding (dropping partially-chewed feed). The standard preventive approach is an annual oral exam and **float** (rasping the sharp points) by an equine veterinarian or a certified equine dentist working under veterinary supervision.

The schedule:

- **Young horses (1–4 years)** — exam and float every 6 months; deciduous-tooth (cap) management plus wolf-tooth extraction if needed.
- **Adult horses (5–15 years)** — annual exam and float; most adult horses need a routine float every 12 months.
- **Senior horses (15+ years)** — annual exam, sometimes more frequent. Older teeth wear out, fracture, and are lost; the diet has to follow (see the senior section of the deeper nutrition reference).

Sedation is standard for dental work in horses — a thorough oral exam and float requires the horse to stand quietly with the mouth open for an extended period. Sedation done by a vet is safe and routine.

**EOTRH (equine odontoclastic tooth resorption and hypercementosis)** is a painful progressive condition of the incisors increasingly recognised in older horses. Symptoms: reluctance to bite into carrots, head-tossing at the bit, visible bulging or recession at the gum line of the incisors. Diagnosis is dental radiographs; treatment is extraction of affected incisors. Most older horses with appropriate post-extraction management do very well.
