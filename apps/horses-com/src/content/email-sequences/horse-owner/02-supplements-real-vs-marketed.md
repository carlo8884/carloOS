The equine supplement market in the US is a multi-billion-dollar category. Some of those products do exactly what the bag claims. Many do not. Telling them apart requires reading the published equine veterinary literature, not the marketing copy.

A short summary of where the evidence sits, by category.

---

**Joint supplements (glucosamine, chondroitin, hyaluronic acid, MSM)**

The most-bought equine supplement category. The published evidence is mixed but useful:

- **Oral glucosamine and chondroitin** show modest bioavailability and modest measurable joint-fluid effects in published studies (*Equine Veterinary Journal* and the *Journal of Equine Veterinary Science*). The effect size is real but smaller than the marketing implies. Reasonable as a supportive part of a joint-health program in older or hard-working horses; not a substitute for veterinary diagnosis of lameness.
- **Injectable joint products** (Adequan, Legend) have substantially stronger published evidence than oral supplements. These require a vet prescription and administration.
- **MSM** is widely supplemented; the evidence base is thinner than for glucosamine/chondroitin. Generally well-tolerated.

The honest position: oral joint supplements are a supportive tool, not a treatment for a diagnosed joint problem.

**Hoof supplements (biotin, zinc, methionine)**

The strongest evidence base of any supplement category. **20 mg biotin daily**, combined with zinc and methionine, is the dose discussed in NRC and in the published equine literature for hoof-quality support. Effect takes 6–12 months to be visible because the hoof grows slowly — a hoof supplement is a multi-cycle commitment, not a quick fix. Will not correct a structural problem that belongs to the farrier.

**Gut / hindgut supplements (probiotics, prebiotics, yeast culture)**

Evidence is improving but still inconsistent. Yeast culture (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) has the best-supported track record for hindgut fermentation support; probiotic strains vary widely in viability after the manufacturing-and-shelf process. Reasonable adjuncts for horses with documented gastrointestinal dysbiosis; not a substitute for forage-first feeding (see the previous email in this sequence).

**Vitamin-mineral / ration balancer**

Most useful supplement category for horses on stored hay. Vitamin E is the standout — fresh pasture is high in vitamin E, stored hay loses it over weeks to months. Horses without pasture access are commonly vitamin-E deficient. Selenium varies dramatically by region; some areas are deficient, some are excess (selenium toxicity is real). Test before aggressive supplementation.

A ration balancer fed at the manufacturer's recommended intake is the cheapest way to deliver a balanced vitamin-mineral package and avoids the over-supplementation risk of adding individual supplements one at a time.

**Calming supplements, weight-gain supplements, "ulcer" supplements**

Categories where the marketing routinely runs ahead of the evidence. A calming supplement does not substitute for addressing pain, fit, or training; a weight-gain supplement does not substitute for adequate forage; an "ulcer supplement" does not substitute for omeprazole-class treatment of a diagnosed ulcer. Defer to the equine vet for actual diagnosis-and-treatment.

---

**The honest rule:** if a supplement claim sounds clinical, look for the supporting evidence in the equine veterinary literature, not on the bag. The bag has not been to vet school.
