Most horse-owner energy goes to grain — which sweet feed, which performance brand, which senior formula. Most of the leverage is in forage. The 100-foot horse gastrointestinal tract is built for near-continuous fibrous intake; the small stomach produces acid continuously whether food is present or not. Long gaps between hay feedings are the most common preventable driver of ulcers, hindgut dysbiosis, colic, and behavioural problems under saddle.

---

**The minimum**

The NRC *Nutrient Requirements of Horses* (sixth revised edition) sets minimum forage at 1% of body weight in dry matter and recommends 1.5–2% for normal maintenance. Most working horses sit at the upper end. For a 1,000-lb horse, that's 15–20 lb of hay per day, minimum.

But intake alone is not the metric. **Continuity is the metric.** A horse that inhales 20 lb of hay in 4 hours and then stands with nothing in the stomach for the next 8 hours is at elevated ulcer risk regardless of total daily intake. The published equine veterinary literature on equine gastric ulcer syndrome (EGUS) — in journals such as the *Equine Veterinary Journal* and the *Journal of Equine Veterinary Science* — has repeatedly documented the relationship between long feed-free intervals and EGUS prevalence, particularly in performance horses.

**The single highest-leverage tool is a slow-feeder hay net** — small mesh (1–1.5″ openings) that extends the time a horse takes to consume the same quantity by 2–4×. Most stalled or paddocked horses can effectively eat continuously by combining a slow-feeder hay net with multiple feedings.

---

**Hay quality basics**

- **Grass hay** (timothy, orchard, bermuda, brome) — the foundation forage for most horses. Lower energy and protein than legume hay; appropriate for easy keepers, maintenance, and weight-gain-prone horses. Excellent for ad libitum slow-feeder use.
- **Legume hay** (alfalfa, clover) — higher protein, calcium, and energy. Appropriate for hard keepers, heavy work, growing youngsters, lactating mares, and seniors that struggle to maintain weight. Not appropriate as the primary forage for overweight or laminitis-prone horses.
- **Mixed hay** — often the practical compromise: more palatable than pure grass, less calorie-dense than pure legume.

**Testing hay.** A hay analysis from a forage testing lab is $20–40 and tells you the actual protein, energy, NSC (non-structural carbohydrate), and mineral content of the specific batch in your barn. It is the foundation of any precise ration design and the only way to confirm a hay is safe for a metabolic horse.

---

**When grain enters the picture**

Most horses in light-to-moderate work on good quality hay do not need grain. The decision to add grain should be driven by body condition (the 1–9 Henneke scale; a working horse sits at 5–6) and by work level — not by tradition. When grain is needed, commercial balanced feeds (Purina, Triple Crown, Nutrena) are preferable to straight grains because they are formulated with the vitamins and minerals whole grains lack.
