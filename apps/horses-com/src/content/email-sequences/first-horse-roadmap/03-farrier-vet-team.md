Two of the five most important relationships in horse ownership are professional ones: your vet and your farrier. The barn picks itself up over time. These two you choose deliberately.

**Farrier cadence.** A typical healthy adult horse is on a **6-week trim or shoeing cycle**. Some horses with fast growth or specific shoeing needs run 4–5; some older horses on careful management hold at 7–8. The single most common preventable hoof problem in first-horse ownership is *cycle drift* — letting "this week" become "next week" become "we&apos;ll catch it next time". Long toe and low heel develop fast; the corrective trim that follows is uncomfortable for the horse and disproportionately expensive.

Book the next appointment at the current appointment. Standing.

**Vet cadence (healthy adult).**

- **Semi-annual wellness visit** — spring and fall. Vaccinations on AAEP-recommended schedule (core: tetanus, EEE/WEE, West Nile, rabies; risk-based: influenza, EHV, strangles, others). Coggins test annually (required for interstate travel). Fecal egg count to guide deworming.
- **Annual dental.** Floating, mouth check, balance assessment. Younger and senior horses sometimes need this more often.
- **As needed.** Lameness work-ups, emergency calls, foaling, breeding work.

Seniors, broodmares, and performance horses see more. Your vet builds your specific calendar.

**How to vet-shop.**

- Start with the **AAEP "Find a Vet" search** at aaep.org. AAEP membership is not the only credential, but it signals continuing education and equine focus.
- Ask the barn manager and at least two boarders unrelated to the manager.
- Confirm the practice handles **emergencies** — own after-hours service, or a clearly named referral hospital and process. "Call this number" is fine; "we don&apos;t really do emergencies" is not.
- Schedule the first non-emergency visit early. Walk the vet to your horse, share your PPE notes, ask what they&apos;d watch in this horse over the next year.

**How to farrier-shop.**

- Ask your vet who they refer to. Vets watch hoof outcomes over thousands of horses; they have opinions.
- Read *American Farriers Journal* on the standards conversation if you want background. It is the trade publication; what your farrier says should be recognizable against what the journal discusses.
- Watch a farrier work *before* committing to them. Quiet horses, clean shoeing or trim, hoof balance after, no shortcuts under time pressure.
- Cost is a poor proxy for skill. Reputation among vets and long-tenure clients is the better signal.

**How to be a client they pick up the phone for at 11 p.m.**

- Be ready: vitals taken, environment described, photos if a wound, exact timeline of onset.
- Be honest: about what you tried, what you fed, what you missed.
- Pay your invoices.
- Don&apos;t cry wolf. Ask "is this something I should worry about" by text or scheduled call; save the 11 p.m. number for actual emergencies.

— Horses.com Editorial
