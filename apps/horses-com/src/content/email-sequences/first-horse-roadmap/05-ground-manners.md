If you&apos;ve been at this about five weeks, the honeymoon is over and you&apos;ve started to notice the small things your horse does that the previous owner either trained out or quietly tolerated.

Now is when ground manners get installed for the long haul. Not in a clinic. Not in a 90-minute session once a week. In **two-minute reps, every time you handle the horse**.

**The five non-negotiables.**

1. **Whoa.** Stop where I say, stay stopped until I cue forward. Drill at the halter from day one. A horse who breaks whoa when tied, when held for the vet, or in the cross-ties is a horse who will eventually hurt someone.
2. **Walk on.** Step forward on a verbal cue, with light lead pressure. The horse should not drag you, and should not require you to drag them.
3. **Back.** Step back, one foot at a time, on a gentle pressure cue (chest, halter, or rope). Critical for unloading from trailers, for moving the horse off you in tight spaces, and for any moment a horse is crowding.
4. **Over.** Move the hindquarters or shoulders sideways off a pressure cue. The single most useful safety skill in a stall and around farriers.
5. **Stand.** Stand still, head down, while you groom, tack up, hold a hoof, take a temperature, or chat with the farrier. Standing is a *trained* behavior, not a default.

**Respecting personal space.** A horse who walks into you, leans on you, or "checks" you with their shoulder has not been corrected. The fix is geometric, not punitive: stake your space (an imaginary 18-inch bubble around you), and ask the horse to move out of it consistently and quietly. Use the over cue. Be predictable. Repeat.

**Picking up feet.** Four feet, every time, on a verbal cue ("foot") and a slight squeeze on the chestnut or fetlock. Hold. Set the foot down gently — never let the horse pull it back. If your horse will not pick up a foot for you, your farrier&apos;s job becomes dangerous, and they will eventually charge accordingly or stop coming.

**Standing tied.** A horse who pulls back, paws, or weaves while tied is broadcasting that tying was never installed well. Quick releases, the right tying location, and gradual extension of tied time (start with one minute, end with twenty) all matter. If you have a chronic puller, consult a trainer — solo experimentation is how horses break halters and humans break collarbones.

**The reps that actually work.**

- **Short.** Two to three minutes per session, several times a day. Not 30-minute "training sessions".
- **Inside the routine.** Cue stand while you brush. Cue back as you bring the horse out of the stall. Cue over as you scoop manure past them. The training is the day; the day is the training.
- **Calm tools.** A regular halter, a 10–12 ft lead rope, a quiet voice. No dressage whip used as a club, no chains under the chin on a horse who does not need them.
- **Consistent cues.** Pick a word. Use only that word for that cue. Everyone who handles your horse uses the same words. The horse needs the cue to mean exactly one thing, every time.

**What good looks like at 90 days.** A horse who walks calmly off a verbal cue, halts within two strides, backs straight, picks up all four feet, stands quietly for the farrier and vet, and can be left tied for 20 minutes without drama. None of those skills are remarkable. All of them are trained.
