If you have not bought the horse yet — read this twice.

The pre-purchase exam (PPE) is the highest-leverage single expense in horse ownership. A typical PPE in the US runs roughly $300–$700 for the base exam, scaling up with imaging. Against a $5,000–$50,000 purchase plus tens of thousands of dollars in lifetime care, the PPE is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

**What a thorough base PPE includes:**

- **History review.** Age, sex, registration, prior injury, prior soundness work, deworming history, vaccination history, current feed.
- **Identification check.** Markings, microchip scan, registration papers cross-checked. (You&apos;d be surprised.)
- **Eye exam.** Penlight and ophthalmoscope. Uveitis, cataracts, asymmetry.
- **Dental and mouth exam.** Wolf teeth, hooks, asymmetric wear.
- **Cardiopulmonary auscultation.** Heart and lungs at rest. Murmur, arrhythmia, respiratory noise.
- **Skin, conformation, and palpation.** Head to tail. Old scars matter; new heat matters more.
- **Hoof exam.** Sole testers, balance, shoe wear pattern. A skilled farrier&apos;s eye supplements the vet&apos;s.
- **Lameness and gait evaluation.** In hand at walk and trot, on a straight line and small circle, both directions. Hard and soft surfaces if available.
- **Flexion tests.** Each limb&apos;s major joints flexed, then trotted off. Positive flexion is not a death sentence — it&apos;s a flag to investigate.
- **Neurological screen.** Tail tone, tail pull, backing, circling, slope work. Subtle ataxia is easy to miss without it.

**What to add as the price climbs:**

- **Radiographs.** A typical "survey" set for a sport prospect is feet, hocks, stifles, fetlocks — sometimes more. For a low-cost trail prospect, feet at minimum.
- **Ultrasound.** When palpation or imaging flags soft-tissue concern.
- **Blood panel + drug screen.** The drug screen exists because sellers occasionally administer NSAIDs or sedatives that mask lameness or behavior. Worth it on any horse you don&apos;t know personally.
- **Endoscopy.** If respiratory noise is heard or if the horse is sold as upper-level athletic.

**Who picks the vet.** *You* pick the vet. Not the seller&apos;s vet. The AAEP (American Association of Equine Practitioners) maintains a public "Find a Vet" search at aaep.org — start there and confirm the vet has no relationship with the seller.

**What "passing" means.** PPEs are not pass/fail. A PPE produces findings and a risk assessment relative to *your intended use*. A horse with mild navicular changes may be a problem for a jumper and a non-issue for a trail mount. The vet&apos;s job is to describe findings; your job is to decide what you can live with.

**The honest answer the seller doesn&apos;t volunteer.** If the seller resists a thorough PPE, walk away. The horses that pass PPEs with caveats you can live with are out there.

— Horses.com Editorial
