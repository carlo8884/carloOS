You&apos;re past day 50. The horse is settled, the farrier&apos;s been once or twice, the feed program is steady. This is when the equine first-aid kit gets built — *before* you need it, not at the moment something happens at 9 p.m. on a Sunday.

**The kit, itemized.**

- **Digital thermometer** (large-animal, with cord and clip)
- **Stethoscope** — a basic single-head model is fine
- **Antiseptic solution** — chlorhexidine 2% or povidone-iodine ("Betadine")
- **Saline solution** — sterile, for flushing wounds and eyes
- **Vet wrap** (Co-Flex, Coban, generic equivalents) — at least six rolls
- **Sterile gauze pads** — 4×4 and larger
- **Non-stick wound dressings** (Telfa pads)
- **Roll cotton** and **brown gauze** (for standing bandages and pressure wraps)
- **Standing-wrap quilts and polo wraps** — two sets, clean
- **Hoof pick + hoof knife**
- **EMT gel** or comparable wound dressing
- **Triple antibiotic ointment** (no painkillers — those are absorbed and complicate vet care)
- **Bandage scissors** — blunt-tip
- **Hemostats** and a pair of curved scissors
- **Disposable gloves**
- **Headlamp** with fresh batteries
- **Notebook + pen** for vitals
- **Banamine (flunixin)** — *only if your vet has prescribed it and instructed you on when to use it.* Banamine masks symptoms; the wrong dose at the wrong time can complicate a colic call.
- **A printed contact card.** Your vet&apos;s main and emergency line, the referral hospital&apos;s 24-hour line, the closest equine-experienced trailer if you don&apos;t have your own, the barn manager.

A modest kit can be assembled for $150–$250 from any tack store or equine supply retailer. **Normal vital ranges for an adult horse at rest.**

- **Heart rate:** 28–44 beats per minute
- **Respiratory rate:** 8–16 breaths per minute
- **Temperature:** 99.0–101.5°F (37.2–38.6°C)
- **Capillary refill time** (gums): under 2 seconds
- **Gut sounds:** present and varied in all four quadrants
- **Mucous membranes** (gums): moist, salmon pink

Take these at rest, in the stall or paddock, before you tack up or move the horse. Do it weekly until it&apos;s reflex.

**Call the vet immediately.**

- Suspected colic with elevated heart rate (>60 bpm), no gut sounds, repeated rolling, sweating, distressed posture
- Severe lameness — non-weight-bearing on a limb
- Active bleeding that does not slow with direct pressure within a few minutes
- Wounds near a joint or tendon sheath
- Eye injuries — any opacity, discharge, or squinting (eye problems escalate fast)
- Choke (esophageal obstruction) — nasal discharge of food, drooling, distressed swallowing
- Suspected laminitis — heat in hooves, increased digital pulses, shifting weight, classic "leaning back" stance
- Foaling complications
- Snake bite, severe allergic reaction, or trauma

**Monitor and call in the morning.**

- Mild lameness on a previously sound horse, no swelling, no heat
- Small skin lacerations away from joints, easily controlled
- Slight off-feed for one meal in an otherwise normal horse
- One slightly soft manure pile in an otherwise normal horse

**Write the emergency plan and post it on the wall.** One page, in the tack room or feed room.

- Vet primary number + after-hours number
- Referral hospital name, address, 24-hour line
- Closest equine trailer + whose phone number it lives on
- Closest barn manager + your phone number
- The horse&apos;s ID: registered name, age, color, identifying markings, microchip number
- Insurance company + policy number (if applicable)
- Your vet&apos;s instructions for banamine or other PRN meds

When you&apos;re standing in the aisle at 11 p.m. trying to remember the after-hours number, you do not want to be opening four apps. Print it. Stick it up. Update it twice a year.
