If your horse has been home about three weeks, the feed transition window is right now.

**The cardinal rule: gradual changes.** The equine gut is colonized by bacteria adapted to whatever the horse was eating last week. Switch feed abruptly and the population crashes, releasing endotoxins as it does. The result ranges from loose manure to colic to laminitis. The fix is mechanical, not exotic: change feed gradually, by about **10% replacement of the old feed per day**, completing the transition over **roughly two weeks**. Slower for older horses, sensitive horses, or horses with a colic history.

This applies to hay, too. New hay supply? Same rule. Mix the new and old hay.

**Forage first.** A healthy adult horse should be eating **1.5–2.5% of body weight in forage per day** (dry matter), as a baseline. For a 1,100 lb horse, that&apos;s roughly 16–27 lb of hay per day, depending on activity level and pasture access. Forage is not the "background" — it is the main meal. Concentrates fill nutritional gaps; they do not replace hay.

**Assessing hay quality.** You will pick up hundreds of bales over the next decade. Develop the eye now.

- **Color.** Green is good; bleached straw-yellow on the outside is fine if the inside is green; brown or black is mold or heat damage — refuse.
- **Smell.** Sweet and grassy is good. Musty, sour, or chemical is mold or fermentation — refuse.
- **Dust.** A small amount of leaf dust is normal. A cloud when you break a flake is respirable particulates that aggravate equine asthma. Soak or steam dusty hay, or change supply.
- **Stem-to-leaf ratio.** More leaf = more nutrients and palatability. Excess stem = late-cut, lower-quality forage.
- **Foreign matter.** No bailing twine inside the bale. No dead mice. No blister beetles in alfalfa (toxic). No prickly weeds.

If you can, ask your hay supplier for a **forage analysis** — a lab report giving you crude protein, digestible energy, NSC (non-structural carbohydrate), calcium, phosphorus, and more. For a metabolic or laminitis-prone horse, this is non-negotiable.

**When concentrates make sense.**

- **Hard keepers** who can&apos;t maintain weight on forage alone, especially seniors with poor dentition.
- **Working horses** in moderate-to-hard work where forage alone leaves them short on calories.
- **Specific deficiencies** flagged by a forage analysis or vet assessment — most commonly vitamin E, selenium (region-dependent), and salt.
- **Ration balancers** — a low-volume, vitamin-and-mineral-fortified pellet — for easy keepers on good forage. These deliver micronutrients without calorie load.

**When concentrates are a tax.** Easy keepers on good pasture, getting "a scoop of sweet feed" because that&apos;s what the previous owner did. You are paying for and feeding sugar your horse does not need; ulcer and laminitis risk climbs in proportion.

**Body condition score (BCS), not the bowl.** The Henneke 1–9 BCS scale is the standard. Aim for 5/9 for most pleasure horses; performance disciplines vary. Re-score every 4–6 weeks. If you can&apos;t see a faint outline of ribs but can feel them easily, you&apos;re close. The bowl tells you what you fed; the body tells you what worked.

**The transition checklist for the next two weeks:**

1. Decide whether you&apos;re keeping the previous owner&apos;s feed plan or moving to your own. Pick one and commit; don&apos;t drift.
2. If switching, blend the old and new feed and increase the new portion ~10% per day.
3. Weigh hay, do not eyeball flakes (flakes vary wildly).
4. Record water intake. A horse on hay-only forage drinks more than one on pasture — make sure the trough never runs dry.
5. Body-condition score now, and again at week 6.
