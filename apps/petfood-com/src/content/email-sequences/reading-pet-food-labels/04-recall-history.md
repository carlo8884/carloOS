Pet food recalls happen at a regular cadence. Some are voluntary withdrawals at the manufacturer's initiative; some are FDA-coordinated; some are state-level. The mechanism is consistent enough that you can build a habit of checking before you switch foods, and a small habit pays off the first time it catches a recall on the bag in your pantry.

**The primary database.**

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's Center for Veterinary Medicine (CVM) maintains the **Recalls & Withdrawals** index at fda.gov. It covers animal-product recalls across pet food, treats, supplements, and veterinary therapeutic diets. The index lists product name, brand, lot codes, recall date, hazard, distribution, and the recall classification (Class I = reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences or death; Class II = temporary or medically reversible adverse consequences with low probability of serious harm; Class III = unlikely to cause adverse health consequences).

A search for a brand name returns every recall the FDA has classified involving that brand. The corresponding manufacturer is often listed; this matters because a single manufacturer may run multiple brands, and a recall affecting one brand may signal a process or supply-chain issue affecting siblings. Champion Petfoods (Acana, Orijen), Diamond Pet Foods (multiple private labels), Mars Petcare (Pedigree, Iams, Royal Canin, Cesar, Eukanuba), Nestlé Purina (Purina One, Pro Plan, Dog Chow, Fancy Feast), and Hill's Pet Nutrition (Science Diet, Prescription Diet) are the major manufacturers; the brand-to-manufacturer map is rarely visible on the bag itself.

A second useful index is the AVMA's recall list, which mirrors FDA notices with an animal-health-professional perspective. Several state Departments of Agriculture publish parallel notices. PetFood.com maintains per-brand recall pages that aggregate these sources into one timeline per brand; we link to FDA primary sources rather than substituting for them.

**The four common causes.**

Looking at FDA CVM recalls over the past decade, four hazard categories account for the majority of incidents:

1. **Salmonella and Listeria contamination.** Most often associated with raw and freeze-dried products, jerky treats, and some dry kibbles where a process failure allowed pathogen survival or post-pasteurization contamination. Risk profile: bacterial gastrointestinal illness in pets; in immunocompromised, very young, or elderly humans, cross-contamination from the bowl or hands can produce systemic disease. The CDC and FDA jointly note this household-transmission risk for raw foods.

2. **Mycotoxins.** Aflatoxin, vomitoxin (deoxynivalenol), and fumonisin are fungal toxins that contaminate stored grains under specific moisture conditions. The 2020–2021 aflatoxin contamination of multiple Sportmix and related Midwestern Pet Foods products produced one of the most severe recent recalls, with FDA confirmed canine fatalities. Mycotoxin testing is part of standard quality assurance for the larger manufacturers; the variability sits at the smaller end of the market.

3. **Vitamin or mineral excess.** Vitamin D toxicity recalls have recurred — the 2018–2019 incidents involving multiple brands (Sunshine Mills lines, Hill's Science Diet/Prescription Diet canned, and others) traced to premix formulation errors. Vitamin D excess produces hypercalcemia and acute kidney injury. Vitamin A excess and thiamine deficiency (the opposite — too little) have also driven recalls, including a multi-brand canned-cat-food thiamine-deficiency series.

4. **Foreign material.** Metal fragments, plastic pieces, bone shards. Usually voluntary, usually Class II. Low-frequency but consistent — these recalls are an artifact of the physical complexity of the manufacturing line, not necessarily a sign of broader process failure.

A fifth, lower-frequency category includes adulteration (the 2007 melamine episode, which originated in imported wheat gluten and protein concentrates and produced acute renal failure in many cats and dogs), pentobarbital residues (from rendered ingredients of undisclosed origin — the Evanger's and Gravy Train cases in 2017–2018), and Salmonella outbreaks tied to specific treat lots.

**What to do if your food is recalled.**

The procedure is straightforward and identical for almost every recall:

1. **Find the lot code.** It is printed on the bag, can, or pouch, usually near the best-by date. The recall notice specifies which lot codes are affected — not every package of the brand is necessarily recalled.

2. **Stop feeding it.** Even if your pet has shown no symptoms. The recall is the warning, not the symptom.

3. **Take the bag to your retailer for a refund.** Almost every retailer honors recall returns without receipt for current product.

4. **Monitor your pet for symptoms appropriate to the hazard.** For Salmonella: lethargy, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, dehydration. For mycotoxin: jaundice, vomiting, lethargy, anorexia, dark urine. For vitamin D excess: increased thirst and urination, vomiting, weight loss. The recall notice usually includes the symptom panel.

5. **If you see symptoms, call your veterinarian.** Bring the lot code. Save a sample of the food if possible — the FDA's adverse event reporting (Safety Reporting Portal) accepts owner-submitted reports, and a lab-quantifiable sample is more useful than the report alone.

6. **Switch to a food made by a different manufacturer.** Not just a different brand — manufacturer matters more than brand for recall correlation. If the recall is mycotoxin-related on a co-packed brand, a sibling brand from the same plant may carry the same risk even if it has not yet been recalled.

**Building the habit.**

Two recommendations. First, sign up for FDA CVM recall alerts at fda.gov — it is a free email subscription, and you will see recalls before they hit social media or the morning news. Second, the first time you start feeding a new brand, take five minutes to look up the brand's recall history on the FDA database. Three recalls in five years is a different signal than zero recalls in fifteen years; both happen.
