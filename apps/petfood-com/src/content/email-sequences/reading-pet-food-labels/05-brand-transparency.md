A pet food bag carries about a dozen pieces of legally required information, and a few dozen pieces of optional marketing copy. None of the panels are obligated to tell you who formulated the food, where the ingredients were sourced, where the food was manufactured, what the quality assurance process looks like, or whether feeding trials were conducted on the actual formula (versus a "family" lead product). For all of that, you have to ask the manufacturer directly — and most manufacturers are equipped to answer, if you ask the right questions.

The standard reference is the **World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA) Global Nutrition Committee's recommendations on selecting pet foods**, sometimes called the "WSAVA Guidelines" or the "WSAVA 8-Point Questionnaire." It is a short owner-facing handout published by an international veterinary body, with no commercial sponsorship from pet food manufacturers, that consolidates what to ask. The wording below is adapted; the original handout is linked at the bottom.

**The eight questions.**

1. **Do you employ a full-time qualified nutritionist?** "Qualified" in WSAVA's framing means a board-certified veterinary nutritionist (Dip. ACVIM — Nutrition, or Dip. ECVCN) or a PhD in animal nutrition. "Has a vet on the advisory board" is not the same answer. "Works with consultants" is not the same answer. A staff veterinary nutritionist signs off on every formula; a consultant reviews quarterly. The difference matters when a problem appears in production and someone has to make a same-day call on a substitution.

2. **Who formulates your diets, and what are their credentials?** Names and credentials, not job titles. If the manufacturer is unwilling to name the formulator, that is a data point. Smaller brands sometimes outsource formulation to consulting firms (Pet Food Experts, BSM Partners, AFB International, et al.) — that is not a defect, but the question is whether the consulting firm employs board-certified personnel and how the manufacturer's QA validates the consultant's work.

3. **Are your diets tested using AAFCO feeding trials, or are they formulated to meet AAFCO Nutrient Profiles?** This is the same question last week's email asked you to find on the bag — but the manufacturer's answer often adds detail the bag does not. A "family-tested" formula may carry a feeding-trial AAFCO statement on the bag because a structurally similar sibling was trialed; the actual SKU you are feeding may not have been. The question disambiguates.

4. **Where are your foods produced and manufactured? Can the facility be visited?** The actual physical plant, not the headquarters address. Co-packing — where a brand outsources manufacturing to a third-party plant that also produces competing brands — is common. Diamond Pet Foods, CJ Foods, ANI Tianjin (for imports), Ohio Pet Foods, and others run multiple-client plants. A brand willing to disclose its plant and its co-packing arrangement is operating differently from a brand that treats the question as proprietary.

5. **What specific quality control measures do you use to assure the consistency and quality of your ingredients and the end product?** What you want to hear: HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) program, defined supplier-approval program with supplier audits, incoming-ingredient testing for mycotoxins / heavy metals / pathogens, in-line process controls, finished-product testing, retained samples, and a defined release-to-distribution gate. What you may hear instead: a generic "we have rigorous standards" answer with no specifics. The specifics differ across manufacturers and are the most useful signal.

6. **Will you provide a complete nutrient analysis of your best-selling dog or cat food?** Not just the guaranteed analysis from the bag — the full assayed nutrient profile against the AAFCO nutrient panel. A manufacturer that runs full assays as standard QA will have this on file and can send a PDF. A manufacturer that has only the guaranteed analysis is doing less assay work.

7. **What is the caloric value per gram, can, or cup of your foods?** This is on the bag (since 2014, under FDA requirements). The question is here in the WSAVA list because the manufacturer's customer-service answer is a calibration of how oriented the brand is to nutritional support questions vs. sales support questions. A nutrition-oriented brand can also tell you the caloric distribution by macronutrient (% kcal from protein, % from fat, % from carbohydrate), which is more useful for any therapeutic context than the per-cup number alone.

8. **What kind of product research has been conducted? Are the results published in peer-reviewed journals?** Some manufacturers run substantial published research programs — Royal Canin's clinical research database, Hill's published studies on therapeutic diets, Purina's joint research with universities. Most boutique brands run no published research. Lack of published research is not the same as lack of evidence, but it shifts where the available evidence lives — independent academic literature vs. manufacturer-internal data the public cannot read.

**How to ask.**

Customer service email or the contact form on the manufacturer's website. The WSAVA questionnaire is well known enough inside the industry that most large manufacturers will recognize it on sight and have a prepared response. Expect a same-week reply from the major brands. A two-week non-reply, or a "this information is proprietary" reply, is itself the answer.

A practical compression: if a manufacturer answers questions 1, 4, and 5 substantively, the brand is operating in line with WSAVA's framing. If the manufacturer answers none of them substantively, the brand may still be making a perfectly good food — boutique brands are not categorically worse — but the evidence base for that food is thinner than the marketing language suggests.

**Where this fits in PetFood.com's scoring.**

Our methodology rubric scores brand transparency as a weighted input. The eight WSAVA questions map to four scored dimensions: nutritionist employment, manufacturing transparency, QA program detail, and published research. We do not score brands on which answers they give — we score them on whether they answer at all, and whether the answer is substantive. A brand can score well on transparency while making a food we rank middle on completeness, and vice versa. The two dimensions are separately tracked.

**What to do this week.**

Pick the brand you feed most often. Send the customer service team a short email with three questions (probably 1, 4, and 5). Note the response time and the substance of the reply. Repeat next month with a second brand if you are comparing. Over a year you will have built your own private brand-transparency dataset that no marketing campaign can paper over.
