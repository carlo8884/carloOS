Final email in the series.

If you've worked through the previous seven, you have a working framework: read the AAFCO statement first, decode the product-name rule, scan the ingredient list for splitting and species-named vs. unnamed by-product, convert the guaranteed analysis to dry matter before comparing brands, check the recall history, and treat marketing claims as advertising rather than evidence. That framework is the deliverable.

Most of what comes next is repetition. Each bag you read is faster than the last. The library below is the set of PetFood.com reference pages that go deeper on the topics this sequence raised; bookmark the ones you'll come back to.

**External references the site relies on**

- **FDA Center for Veterinary Medicine** — Pet Food Labels guidance and Recalls & Withdrawals index (fda.gov). The authoritative source for U.S. labeling rules and current recall status. Sign up for FDA CVM recall email alerts; they are free.
- **AAFCO Official Publication** — the underlying model regulations every state pet food law derives from. The 2025 OP includes the updated Dog and Cat Food Nutrient Profiles. Access is paid; most of what owners need is summarized in our guides and in FDA's free CVM material.
- **WSAVA Global Nutrition Committee** — the "Recommendations on Selecting Pet Foods" owner handout (the 8-point questionnaire from week 5) and the broader Global Nutrition Guidelines for veterinary professionals.
- **Tufts Petfoodology** — the Cummings School of Veterinary Medicine's owner-facing nutrition writing, including the Freeman group's coverage of the DCM investigation. Independent of pet food industry funding.
- **NRC Nutrient Requirements of Dogs and Cats (2006)** — the reference text underlying the AAFCO profiles. Old but the most rigorous English-language source on the underlying nutritional science.

**One last note on marketing language.**

You will see confident-sounding claims that contradict the framework this sequence built. "Biologically appropriate." "Ancestral." "Human-grade" (which has a narrow legal definition but is broadly misused). "Holistic" (which has no legal definition at all). "Super-premium." "Premium." "Vet-recommended" (which can mean a single veterinarian on the advisory board). These are marketing categories, not evidence categories. None of them appear on the FDA's labeling rule sheet. None of them have an AAFCO definition. When in doubt, the source is the standard — not the volume.
