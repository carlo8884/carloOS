The AAFCO Nutritional Adequacy Statement is one line of small type, usually on the back of the bag near the ingredient list. It is the most important regulatory text on the package — more important than the product name, more important than the ingredient list, more important than every word on the front. Most owners never read it.

It does three things at once: it tells you which life stage the food is intended for, it tells you the species, and it tells you the pathway the manufacturer used to demonstrate nutritional completeness. The pathway matters because the two allowed pathways are not equivalent in evidence strength.

**The two pathways.**

Under AAFCO's 2025 Official Publication, Chapter 6, model regulation PF8, a food sold as "complete and balanced" must be either:

1. **Formulated to meet the AAFCO Dog (or Cat) Food Nutrient Profiles for [life stage].** Calculation only. The manufacturer assembles ingredients on paper, runs the formula against the published nutrient profile, and certifies that every required nutrient meets the minimum (and where applicable, falls below the maximum). No animals are fed the food before it is sold. The food may also be sent to a lab for nutrient assay, but that is not required by the formulation pathway.

2. **Animal feeding tests using AAFCO procedures substantiate that [product] provides complete and balanced nutrition for [life stage].** The manufacturer ran a feeding trial under the AAFCO protocol. For adult maintenance, that is a minimum 26-week study with 8 dogs, monitored on a defined panel of blood values and body condition. For growth and reproduction, the protocols are longer and stricter. The trial demonstrates that the formula actually sustains an animal — not just that the spreadsheet adds up.

A third, hybrid pathway exists: a "family product" feeding trial, where the trial-tested formula is the "lead" product in a line, and structurally similar siblings inherit the certification under defined rules.

**Why feeding trial is the stronger signal.**

Formulation passes any food that meets the calculated profile. It does not catch nutrient interactions (one nutrient blocking absorption of another), it does not catch bioavailability problems (a nutrient present on the label but in a form the animal cannot use), and it does not catch palatability or digestibility failures. Several of the foods implicated in past nutritional adequacy failures — including, historically, taurine-deficiency cardiomyopathy cases and a 2007 melamine adulteration that exploited the crude-protein measurement convention — passed formulation review at the time. Feeding trial catches more of these failures because the animals are the test.

Feeding trial is more expensive (a six-month trial with eight dogs is a meaningful capital commitment), so smaller and boutique brands rarely run them. Larger brands with research budgets — Royal Canin, Purina Pro Plan, Hill's Science Diet, Iams, Eukanuba — run them as a matter of standard practice. This is not an endorsement of those brands across the board; their formulas vary widely in other dimensions we score. It is a comment on which evidence pathway they use.

**"For all life stages" is broader than it sounds.**

A food labeled "complete and balanced for all life stages" is meeting the highest single bar in the AAFCO profile — usually growth and reproduction, which has the most demanding requirements. That sounds like a strong claim, and on its face it is. But it has a quiet consequence: a food formulated to growth-and-reproduction profiles will run higher in calcium, phosphorus, and energy density than a food formulated to adult maintenance. Feeding an all-life-stages food to a sedentary spayed adult dog over years is one of the contributing factors to subclinical weight gain that the Linder and Freeman work at Tufts has documented.

If your dog is an adult, an adult-maintenance certification is the cleaner fit. If you have a puppy, growth or all-life-stages is required. The label is telling you the answer; the AAFCO statement is where the answer is.

**The escape hatch you want to spot.**

If the bag says **"intended for intermittent or supplemental feeding only,"** the food is *not* sold as a complete diet and is not certified to AAFCO profiles. This is common for toppers, treats, broths, single-ingredient "boosters," and many fresh-frozen products. Used as intended — as a topper or supplement — these foods are fine. Used as a sole long-term ration, they are nutritionally incomplete by their own admission. Read this line on every bag in the house before assuming any food in the pantry is sustaining your pet on its own.

**What to do this week.**

Pull the bag (or can) you're feeding right now. Find the AAFCO statement. Confirm three things: species, life stage, and pathway. If the pathway is formulation, that is not a defect — most of the market is formulation. It is a calibration point. If the statement says "intermittent or supplemental feeding only," confirm that the food is supplementing, not replacing, a complete diet.
