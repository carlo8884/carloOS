Every commercial pet food in the U.S. carries a guaranteed analysis panel — four numbers, sometimes more, in a small table on the back of the bag. Most owners glance at the protein percentage, mentally compare it to the protein percentage of another bag, and move on. The comparison is almost always wrong, because the numbers are reported on a basis that is not comparable across formats. This email is the math.

**What the panel reports.**

Under AAFCO Model Regulation PF5 (2025 Official Publication, Chapter 6), the guaranteed analysis panel must report four items at minimum:

- **Crude protein** — minimum guaranteed.
- **Crude fat** — minimum guaranteed.
- **Crude fiber** — maximum guaranteed.
- **Moisture** — maximum guaranteed.

Some states require additional disclosures: ash, calcium, phosphorus, omega-6, omega-3, taurine, glucosamine (when a glucosamine claim is made on the label). The four above are the universal baseline. "Crude" in this context is a chemistry-method descriptor, not a quality descriptor — crude protein is total nitrogen multiplied by a conversion factor (6.25), which is the same method used for human food labels. The 2007 melamine contamination episode was a deliberate exploitation of this measurement: melamine is high in nitrogen, so adding it inflated the reported "protein" without adding any actual protein.

**As-fed vs. dry matter — the comparison trap.**

The percentages on the panel are reported "as-fed." That means they include the moisture in the package — about 10% for a typical kibble, about 78% for a typical canned food. A direct comparison of as-fed protein between a kibble and a can is therefore a comparison of two different denominators.

A worked example. Take a canned food showing 12% crude protein on the panel and 78% moisture. The dry matter — the part of the food that isn't water — is 100% − 78% = 22%. So the protein contribution is:

```
Dry-Matter % = (As-Fed % ÷ (100 − Moisture %)) × 100
             = (12 ÷ 22) × 100
             = 54.5% protein on a dry-matter basis
```

Now take a kibble showing 26% crude protein on the panel and 10% moisture:

```
Dry-Matter % = (26 ÷ (100 − 10)) × 100
             = (26 ÷ 90) × 100
             = 28.9% protein on a dry-matter basis
```

As-fed, the kibble looks like more than twice the protein density (26% vs. 12%). Dry-matter, the can is nearly twice the protein density (54.5% vs. 28.9%). The same arithmetic applies to fat. Any cross-format comparison — kibble vs. canned, kibble vs. fresh, fresh vs. raw, raw vs. dehydrated — must be done on a dry-matter basis before any conclusion is drawn. As-fed comparison is the trap, and most marketing material is happy to let you fall into it.

**Why minimums and maximums don't tell the whole story.**

The panel reports a minimum for protein and fat ("not less than 26%") and a maximum for fiber and moisture ("not more than 4%" / "not more than 10%"). The actual protein content of the food can be substantially above the stated minimum and still be honest labeling. The actual moisture can be below the stated maximum.

What this means in practice: two foods both labeled "26% crude protein minimum" can deliver 26% and 31% respectively in the bag, and both are within the guarantee. The state feed control inspector samples for the *guaranteed* number, not the *actual* number. Several state Departments of Agriculture publish annual feed assay results showing the gap between labeled and assayed values; the gap is usually modest, but it exists and runs in both directions.

The guarantee is a floor, not a measurement. For most purposes the floor is good enough, but it means that comparing two foods at the same labeled "26% protein" line is not comparing two foods at 26% protein. It is comparing two foors that each promise *at least* 26%.

**The missing carbohydrate number.**

Look at any commercial pet food label and you will not find a carbohydrate number. The omission is not an oversight — AAFCO does not require carbohydrate disclosure, and most manufacturers do not volunteer it. For dogs, the absence is inconvenient. For cats — obligate carnivores whose carbohydrate metabolism differs substantially from that of dogs and humans — the absence is a more significant gap.

You can estimate carbohydrate as a residual, often called nitrogen-free extract (NFE) in nutrition literature. The estimate is approximate; it lumps starches, sugars, and some other digestible non-fiber components together; and it assumes the ash content (the mineral residue) is around 6–8% for kibble and 2–3% for canned, which is typical but not universal. The formula:

```
Approx. carbohydrate % = 100 − protein − fat − fiber − moisture − ash
```

For a kibble with 26% protein, 14% fat, 4% fiber, 10% moisture, and ~7% ash, the residual is approximately 39% — meaning roughly 39% of the as-fed weight is carbohydrate. On a dry-matter basis that is about 43%. This is normal for a grain-inclusive adult dog kibble. A "grain-free" kibble that has replaced grains with legumes (peas, lentils, chickpeas) and starchy tubers (potato, tapioca) often has a comparable carbohydrate residual; the marketing language has changed, the macronutrient profile less so.

For a canned cat food with 12% protein, 5% fat, 1% fiber, 78% moisture, and ~2% ash, the residual is approximately 2% — meaning the can is genuinely low in carbohydrate, which is closer to the species' evolutionary intake than the kibble alternative. This is one of the few cases where the label arithmetic supports a clear interpretation.

**What to do this week.**

Pull two bags or cans you compare regularly. Run both through the dry-matter conversion above. If you have been comparing them as-fed, the dry-matter numbers will probably look different from what you expected. For one of them, run the carbohydrate residual. Note the answer somewhere — you will use it the next time you switch brands.
