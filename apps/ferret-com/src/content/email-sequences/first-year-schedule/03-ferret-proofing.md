A ferret-proofed room is not the same as a baby-proofed room. The hazard profile is different. The headline number to keep in mind: gastrointestinal foreign body retrieval is the most common ferret abdominal surgery in pet-ferret clinical practice (Mehler &amp; Bennett, 2001, *JAAHA*). The single largest controllable input on that statistic is what your ferret has access to in the room when you are not watching.

**The 1-inch rule.** If your head fits, the rest of you follows. For a ferret, that geometry holds at roughly 1 inch — any gap, hole, vent, opening, or compressible space larger than 1 inch is a potential exit or entrapment. Walk the room on your hands and knees and look at it from ferret height. Then block:

- Gaps between cabinets and walls
- The space under the dishwasher, refrigerator, and oven
- HVAC vents at floor level (use grates with openings smaller than ½ inch)
- Gaps under and behind couches
- Reclining-furniture mechanisms — see below
- The crack between mattress and box spring
- Crawl spaces and unfinished basement access

**Rubber and foam — the ingestion list.** Ferrets chew rubber and foam and swallow chunks. The foreign body is rarely visible on plain x-ray. The classic surgical case is a ferret that "wasn't eating for two days" who turns out to have a piece of shoe sole or earplug obstructing the gut. Remove from accessible areas:

- Shoes, especially soft soles and insoles
- Earbuds, earplugs, silicone wristbands
- Stress balls, foam toys
- Pencil erasers, rubber bands
- Sneaker rubber, foam slippers, bath mats with foam backing
- Foam packing peanuts (kit/birthday/holiday hazard)
- Some dog and cat toys that ferrets re-purpose

**Reclining furniture.** Recliners and sleeper sofas are statistically a leading cause of mechanical-injury ferret deaths. The ferret sleeps in the mechanism; the owner reclines; the outcome is fatal. Either remove recliners from the ferret room, or develop the habit of checking under and inside the mechanism before each use — every time, no exceptions. This is the one safety habit that does not tolerate "I forgot once."

**Sleep-spot mapping.** Ferrets sleep 18+ hours per day and they pick a small number of preferred spots. Identify yours within the first two weeks and either approve them (line with fleece, mark with toy) or block them. Common favorites: inside a couch cushion, behind a dresser, inside a closet on top of folded laundry, behind the toilet. Mapped sleep spots also help during head-counts.

**The free-roam compromise.** A fully ferret-proofed whole house is rare. The realistic pattern most keepers settle into is one or two fully proofed rooms, with the rest of the house behind doors or baby gates that ferrets cannot squeeze through. This is fine. Ferrets do not need infinite floor space — they need enough proofed space for 3–4 hours of out-of-cage time per day.
