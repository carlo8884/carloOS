Insulinoma is the most common neoplasm reported in domestic ferrets. It is a functional tumor of the pancreatic beta cells that produces inappropriate insulin, causing chronic hypoglycemia. Most ferrets are diagnosed between ages 3 and 7 (Quesenberry &amp; Carpenter, *Ferrets, Rabbits, and Rodents*; *Veterinary Clinics of North America: Exotic Animal Practice*).

You will not see insulinoma in the first year. What you build in the first year is the foundation that lets you catch it early when the watch window opens.

**The early signs to recognize.** They are subtle. Most owners do not connect them to insulinoma until a clinician points it out:

- **Hindlimb weakness or wobble.** The classic sign. A ferret who used to bound up a ramp now hesitates, slips, or has a brief moment where the back end "gives out". Often intermittent at first.
- **Mouth pawing or salivation.** Pawing at the mouth is a ferret sign of nausea, which can be triggered by hypoglycemia. Drooling or hypersalivation has the same root cause.
- **Staring spells / glazed expression.** Brief episodes where the ferret stops moving and stares at nothing, then resumes. These are mild hypoglycemic events.
- **Increased sleep, decreased play.** Ferrets nap a lot at baseline (16–20 hours per day); the warning sign is a change from the individual ferret's normal pattern.
- **Weight loss with maintained appetite,** or weight loss following a brief refusal to eat.

**The fasting blood glucose conversation.** Some exotic-pet vets recommend adding a fasting blood glucose check to the annual exam starting around age 3. A normal ferret fasting glucose is roughly 90–120 mg/dL; insulinoma-suspect ferrets typically show fasting glucose under 60 mg/dL, often into the 30–50 range. This is a vet decision and a vet-collected sample under controlled fasting. The annual visit is the time to raise this conversation with your exotic-pet vet, ideally before symptoms appear.

**Do not blood-glucose test at home.** Human glucometers are not validated for ferrets and frequently produce misleading readings (typically falsely high or wildly variable). More importantly, the diagnosis is not made on a single glucose value — it is made by a vet in clinical context, often with paired insulin levels. A home glucose meter does not help and can mislead you into either complacency or panic. If you suspect symptoms, the right action is "exotic-pet vet visit", not "buy a meter".

**The first-year leverage.** What you do in year one to reduce insulinoma risk:

- **Diet.** Minimize carbohydrate (email 04). The associational evidence in the exotic-pet literature consistently points at chronic carb load as the modifiable risk factor.
- **Weight tracking.** Weekly weights in a notebook from kithood give you the baseline you need to recognize weight loss when it matters.
- **Vet relationship.** The exotic-pet vet who has seen your ferret yearly from age 1 is the person best positioned to recognize subtle changes at age 4.

**One last note.** Insulinoma is treatable — medically with prednisone and diazoxide, surgically by nodulectomy or partial pancreatectomy, often in combination. A diagnosed ferret can live several more years with good quality of life. The reason to catch it early is that early-stage disease responds better to both medical and surgical management. The reason to read about it in year one is that you will recognize the symptoms in year four.
