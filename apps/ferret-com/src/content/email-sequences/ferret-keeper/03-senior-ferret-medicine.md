Average ferret lifespan in captivity is 5–8 years, with some individuals reaching 9–10. The last third of that lifespan — age 4 or 5 onward — is when the disease incidence curve climbs sharply. Three conditions account for the bulk of middle-aged and senior ferret medicine: **insulinoma**, **adrenal disease**, and **lymphoma**. A good senior care program is built around catching these early.

This email is the high-level summary. Treatment decisions belong to an exotic-pet vet; the published reference for each condition lives on the deeper pages on Ferret.com.

---

**Insulinoma — the most common neoplasm in older ferrets**

A functional tumour of the pancreatic beta cells that produces inappropriate insulin. The classic clinical picture: episodes of weakness, drooling, glassy stare, hindlimb ataxia, and seizures — often triggered by exercise or extended periods between meals. Insulinoma is reported as the single most common neoplasm in middle-aged and senior domestic ferrets in the exotic-pet literature.

The leading working hypothesis in the literature: chronic dietary carbohydrate load drives sustained insulin secretion, which over years contributes to beta-cell hyperplasia and eventually insulinoma formation. The evidence is associational rather than experimentally established, but the practical recommendation across exotic-vet sources is consistent: **minimise dietary carbohydrate from kithood onward**, eliminate sugary treats, and use a continuous-grazing food access pattern (not restricted feeding).

Diagnosis is a fasting glucose blood test. Treatment depends on stage and includes medical management (prednisone, diazoxide) and / or surgical (partial pancreatectomy or nodulectomy).

---

**Adrenal disease — the second most common**

Functional tumours or hyperplasia of the adrenal glands producing inappropriate sex hormones. The classic clinical picture: progressive **bilateral symmetric hair loss** (typically starting at the rump or tail base), vulvar swelling in spayed jills, behavioural changes (aggression, restored sex-typical behaviour in neutered animals), itching, and muscle wasting.

The hair-loss pattern is the diagnostic signal. **Any bilateral symmetric alopecia in an adult ferret that is not the spring or fall seasonal moult is adrenal disease until proven otherwise.** Diagnosis is the University of Tennessee adrenal panel (serum hormone assay). Treatment options include surgical removal of the affected gland(s) and / or medical management with deslorelin implants.

---

**Lymphoma — the third common neoplasm**

Variable in presentation. Some ferrets present with a peripheral lymph node enlargement; others with progressive weight loss, lethargy, decreased appetite, and a poor coat. Lymphoma is reported in both juvenile and adult ferrets; the adult presentations are more common in middle age. Diagnosis is biopsy / cytology of an affected lymph node or organ. Treatment is chemotherapy via an exotic-pet oncology referral; prognosis is variable.

---

**The bloodwork cadence**

The standard pattern across exotic-pet practice is:

- **Age 1–3:** annual exam and bloodwork.
- **Age 3–5:** every 6 months.
- **Age 5+:** every 3–6 months — adjusted for the specific ferret's clinical picture.

The screening reasoning: insulinoma is best caught on fasting glucose, adrenal disease on hormone panel, lymphoma on CBC abnormalities. Catching these early changes the prognosis substantially.

---

**What to monitor at home**

- **Weight stability.** One of the most useful single proxies for overall health in middle-aged and senior ferrets. A senior ferret that has lost >10% of body weight in a few months needs a vet visit.
- **Hair loss pattern.** Diffuse seasonal shedding in spring and fall is normal. Bilateral symmetric loss starting at the rump or tail base is not — adrenal panel time.
- **Energy level and appetite.** Episodic weakness, drooling, or unsteadiness signals possible insulinoma episode — fasting glucose test, do not delay.
- **Litter box habits.** Sudden regression in a previously-trained ferret often signals urinary, GI, or systemic disease.
- **Sleep duration vs activity.** Increasing sleep with decreasing alert time is a flag.
