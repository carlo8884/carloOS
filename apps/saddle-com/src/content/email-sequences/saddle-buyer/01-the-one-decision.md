The most common reason riders re-saddle inside two years is that they let brand-fit personality drive the decision and worked backwards into the wrong tree shape for the discipline they actually ride. The correct decision order is the opposite.

**Discipline → tree shape → brand-fit personality.** In that order. Get the first two right and you have a saddle that supports the work for a decade. Get them wrong and no amount of pad work, re-flocking, or fitter intervention fixes it.

---

**Discipline first**

The discipline you ride 80% of the time determines the saddle's geometry. A dressage saddle's deep seat and long, straight flap is the opposite of a jumping saddle's flatter seat and forward, padded flap. An all-purpose saddle is a compromise designed to do neither badly — which is right for the rider who legitimately splits time between schooling on the flat and small jumps, and wrong for the rider who is honest that they really only do one of them.

Be honest about the 80%. The aspirational saddle that fits the discipline you wish you rode is the saddle you re-sell.

**Tree shape second**

Within each discipline, the tree (the internal frame) has to match the horse's back. Specific points that matter, in priority order:

- **Tree width** at the gullet — narrow / medium / wide / extra-wide is a category, not a measurement. Different brands measure it differently; the same "wide" tree can fit a different shoulder slope on a different brand.
- **Tree shape from withers to loin** — flat, curved, or "banana." Most horses are not symmetrically curved and most off-the-rack trees are a generic curve compromise.
- **Panel profile** — the saddle's contact surface with the horse. A panel that matches the back lies flat; one that doesn't bridges or rocks.

A qualified fitter assesses these against the specific horse. The discipline narrows the shortlist; the tree shape narrows it further.

**Brand-fit personality last**

The remaining decision — Stubben vs Bates vs Wintec vs Albion vs Pessoa, etc. — is brand-fit personality. Each brand makes the saddle for its discipline differently: seat-bone width, twist (where the front of the seat narrows under the rider), leather feel, stitch character. Two saddles can have identical specifications and feel completely different to ride.

This is the layer where personal preference is allowed to matter. Brand-fit personality is where the "I tried 12 saddles and one felt right" experience lives.

---

**The cost of inverting the order:** riders who start with "I want a Stubben" or "everyone in my barn rides Bates" choose a brand first and then try to find a tree-width and seat that fit. They end up either over-paying for shimmed correction, or re-selling at a loss inside two years when the compromises catch up.
