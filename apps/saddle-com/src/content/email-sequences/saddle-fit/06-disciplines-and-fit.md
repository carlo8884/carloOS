The twelve points apply to every saddle on every horse. What changes by discipline is the *shape* the points are working through.

A quick tour.

---

**Dressage.** Deep seat, long flap, long billets, straight flap cut to support a long leg position. Fit considerations:

- **Channel width** matters more than in most disciplines. The dressage seat is deeper, the rider's weight sits more directly over the channel, and the horse is being asked to lift through the back and engage the hindquarters — spinal clearance under load is non-negotiable.
- **Balance point** is critical. A dressage saddle that tips even slightly cantle-low throws the rider into a chair seat that the discipline punishes harder than any other.
- **Long billets** mean the girth buckles sit below the saddle flap — the panel-to-girth alignment (point 7) should be checked with the actual dressage girth you ride in, not a jump girth.

**Jumping (close-contact / forward-cut).** Shorter, forward-cut flap; flatter seat; knee-roll support; shorter billets. Fit considerations:

- **Tree angle at the points** (point 5) and **rocker / curve match** (point 11) carry extra weight. The rider is out of the saddle in the gallop and at the fence, putting weight on the pommel area and the tree points — a pinched-points jump saddle creates shoulder restriction at exactly the moment you need free shoulder.
- **Balance point** — jump saddles tend to be pommel-low by design (to put the rider over the horse's centre in two-point). The threshold for "too low" is finer than in dressage.
- **Billet alignment** with a short billet and a jump girth needs re-checking — the geometry is different from a long-billet dressage setup.

**All-purpose (AP).** The compromise saddle — deeper than a jump, less deep than a dressage; forward-cut enough for low fences, straight enough for flatwork. Fit is the same checklist; the compromise is *only* about rider position, not horse fit. A good AP can be a defensible long-term saddle for a horse; a bad one is uncomfortable in both directions.

---

**Western fit — the same principles, different geometry.**

Western saddles are built on a rigid tree with *bars* instead of *panels*, plus a skirt that extends well beyond the tree. The 12-point checklist still applies — only the names and check methods change for a few points:

- **Wither clearance** (point 1) — same check, the gullet of the tree replaces the pommel arch. Need 2–3 fingers of clearance the full length of the wither under girthed tension.
- **Panel contact L+R** (points 2–3) → **bar contact L+R**. Bar angle (the angle of the bar to the horse's back, measured at the front and back of the bar) is the closest Western analogue. Even bar contact, no rocking, no bridging — same logic, harder to read without bar templates.
- **Channel width** (point 4) → **bar spread / gullet width**. Western saddles are specified by gullet width (Semi-QH, Reg-QH, Full-QH, Arab, etc.). Wrong gullet width = wrong bar spread = pinch or rock.
- **Tree angle at the points** (point 5) → **bar angle at the front rigging**. Check by sighting from the side and by hand-feel along the bar.
- **Balance point** (point 6) — same check, just visually shaped by the cantle and the seat dish rather than by the panel curve.
- **Billet alignment** (point 7) → **cinch alignment to the natural girth groove**. Western rigging position (full, 7/8, 3/4, 5/8, centre-fire) is the variable. A 3/4 rigged saddle on a horse with a forward girth groove will pull forward; centre-fire on a horse with a normal groove will pull back. The fitter chooses rigging position to match horse anatomy.
- **Skirt length** is unique to Western. Skirts that extend past the horse's short ribs (last floating rib, plus 2–3 inches of safety margin) can cause lumbar pressure — especially on shorter-backed horses. Short-backed Arabians, gaited horses, and many shorter-coupled Quarter Horses need the *round-skirt* or *shorter-skirt* configuration rather than the standard square skirt.

---

**The two myths to retire.**

**Myth 1: "Western saddles distribute weight so well they don't need fitting like English saddles do."** They distribute weight over a larger surface, which is a virtue. They still need fit checked at every point above. The reason this myth persists is that Western fit problems often present further down the road than English fit problems — chronic, low-grade discomfort rather than acute objection. The horse still suffers.

**Myth 2: "Tree size is a horse-breed thing — Quarter Horse bars for QHs, Full-QH for warmbloods, etc."** Tree size is a *horse-individual* thing. A Quarter Horse can have a Semi-QH back; a Thoroughbred can have a Full-QH back. Use the breed as a starting point, never as the answer.

---

**This week.** If you ride more than one discipline, run the checklist on each saddle. The same horse can be in fit in one saddle and out in another — and the saddle that has been "fine for years" can be the one that quietly drifted.
