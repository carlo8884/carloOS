The question every saddle-owner eventually faces. The honest answer turns on a single distinction: **is the tree wrong, or are the panels just tired?**

---

**The numbers, current US market.**

- **Re-flock** by a credentialed fitter: $150–$400 for an English saddle (varies by panel size, asymmetry to correct, and fitter travel). Done in your barn or theirs; saddle ready the same day or next.
- **Panel replacement** (when re-flocking can't restore the panel): $400–$900. Less common; reserved for hardened or damaged panels.
- **Used English saddle, common quality bands:** $800–$2,500 entry; $2,500–$5,000 mid-market; $5,000–$8,000+ premium.
- **New English saddle, off-the-shelf:** $1,500–$5,500 typical; premium brands $5,500–$8,500.
- **New custom-built English saddle:** $3,500–$7,500, ~3–6 month lead time, sometimes longer.
- **New Western saddle (working / show, quality range):** $1,200–$4,500 working; $3,500–$8,000+ show.

(Specific brand prices change; treat these as 2026 reference bands, not quotes.)

---

**The decision.**

**Re-flock when** all of the following are true:

- The tree is the right width and angle for your horse (your fitter has confirmed, not assumed).
- The tree is structurally sound (no cracks, no twisting, no movement under torsion testing).
- The fit problem is confined to panel feel — lumpy, hard, hollow, or visibly compressed wool.
- The saddle is one you otherwise like — model, balance point, flap cut, seat depth.

Under these conditions, a re-flock returns the saddle to functional fit for $150–$400. There is no reason to spend $5,000 to solve a $250 problem.

**Replace when** any of the following are true:

- The tree is the wrong width or wrong angle for the horse. No amount of flocking corrects a tree mismatch — you can soften it, you can shim it, you cannot fix it.
- The tree is cracked, twisted, or otherwise structurally compromised. This is a buy-and-discard situation, not a repair situation.
- The horse has changed shape significantly (most often: gain or loss of topline, age-related changes, end of growth in a young horse) and the tree no longer matches.
- The saddle was never the right saddle and a re-flock is a delay tactic on the underlying mismatch.

**The hard case in the middle:** the tree is borderline-correct, the panels are tired, the horse has changed a little. This is the case where a credentialed fitter is most useful — they can tell you whether the saddle still has working life with a re-flock or whether you're putting good money into the wrong saddle.

---

**The "saddle every year" check.**

Even the right saddle on the right horse needs an annual re-check. Horses change shape with fitness, age, and season; a saddle that fit twelve months ago may have drifted out of fit without an event you'd notice. This is the SMS and MSA standard recommendation and the AAEP echoes it.

The annual check is not always a re-flock. Often it's a five-minute confirmation that nothing has changed, and you're done. But if you skip it, the first time you find out the saddle has drifted is when behavioural signs appear — and by then you've trained on a fit problem for some months.

---

**The trap to avoid.** "I'll just buy a thicker pad" almost always makes fit worse, not better. A pad cannot compensate for a tree mismatch; in many cases the extra padding *increases* localised pressure by reducing the saddle's surface contact. Sheepskin shims and corrective pads have specific, narrow indications — the fitter prescribes them, you don't troubleshoot them on your own.

---

**This week.** Get out your saddle's purchase receipt or note (or just count years since you last had a fitter look at it). If it's been more than 12 months, book a fitter visit. Not a sales visit — an *assessment* visit. Most fitters offer a flat-fee check independent of any work performed.
