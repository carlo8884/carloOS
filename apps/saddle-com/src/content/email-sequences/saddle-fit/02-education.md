The checklist gives you twelve points. If we had to choose one to dwell on, it would be **panel contact** — and specifically the failure mode called **bridging**.

Bridging is when the front and rear of the panels are in contact with the horse's back but the middle is not. The saddle is, in effect, a two-point seat: pressure concentrated on the shoulders behind the scapula and at the loin in front of the 18th rib. The horse compensates by hollowing the back. The rider compensates by tightening the girth. The horse compensates again by carrying the rider on the forehand. Within a season you have a hollow-backed, forehand-heavy horse who looks "stiff through the topline" — and the saddle is the cause.

The classic test for bridging is to slide a hand under the panel from front to back with the saddle on, girthed loosely. Pressure should be consistent along the panel. If your hand slides freely in the middle and resists at both ends, the saddle is bridging.

Why this gets missed: the static test (no rider, no movement) often hides the bridge. A panel that lies flat at rest can lift off the back the moment the horse strides under it. The dynamic test — saddle girthed properly, horse walking forward, hand checked at the middle of the panel from behind — is the one that catches it.

The fix is rarely the saddle itself; it's the flocking. A skilled flocker can add wool to the central panel and bring contact back. This is craft work. It is also irreversible if done wrong, which is why the right professional matters more than the right brand.
