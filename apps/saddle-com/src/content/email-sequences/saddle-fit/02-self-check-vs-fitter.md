There is a line between what a careful owner can check and what only a credentialed fitter can do. Most fit problems start when somebody crosses that line in the wrong direction.

---

**What you can self-check.** Confidently. Without paying anyone.

- **Routine between-fitter checks.** Every 4–8 weeks, work through points 1–4 of the checklist — wither clearance, panel contact left, panel contact right, channel width. If all four are unchanged from the fitter's last visit, you're fine to keep riding. If anything has shifted, book a re-check before the next month.
- **Sweat-pattern reading after rides.** Point 8 is the rider's most useful long-term tool. Even sweat across the panel = even contact. Dry patches = pressure points. A new dry patch that wasn't there last month is a finding, not a coincidence.
- **Post-ride hair check.** Ruffled hair, broken hair, or new white hair under the saddle area is evidence of pressure. White hairs in particular signal *chronic* damage to the hair follicle and indicate the problem has been present for some time.
- **Behavioural change tracking.** New cinching/girthing objections, new bucks at canter departure, new sourness when tacking. These are flags, not diagnoses. Note when they started and what changed.

That is genuinely a lot. A rider who runs these four checks consistently catches most fit problems before they become injuries.

---

**What only a credentialed fitter can do.** Don't try.

- **Tree-angle assessment with calipers and templates.** Eyeballing the tree points is not the same as measuring the angle against the scapula. The fitter has a wither tracing, a flock pick, an angle gauge.
- **Flock adjustment.** Re-flocking is a craft. Bad flocking introduces asymmetry that was not there before. Even small adjustments require the saddle off the horse, panels opened, wool added or removed in measured amounts, and the panel closed and re-shaped.
- **Tree integrity check.** A cracked or twisted tree may be invisible from the outside. The fitter applies torsion tests to the saddle off the horse to detect movement that shouldn't be there. A cracked-tree saddle is a buy-and-discard situation; you cannot ride it safely under any flocking adjustment.
- **Adjustable-gullet plate selection.** Wintec / Bates / similar systems use interchangeable plates. The plate is chosen by measuring the horse, not by feel.
- **Asymmetric horse compensation.** Almost every horse is asymmetric (handedness in the human rider, dominant hind leg in the horse, riding pattern history). A skilled fitter compensates for the horse's asymmetry within the limits of what the panel can absorb. A rider trying to do this with shims usually makes it worse.

---

**Finding a credentialed fitter.** Two main directories:

- **Society of Master Saddlers (SMS) Qualified Saddle Fitter directory** — mastersaddlers.co.uk. Strong in the UK and Ireland; growing North American presence. SMS QSF is the older, more widely recognised credential.
- **Master Saddlers Association (MSA) Certified Saddle Fitter directory** — mastersaddlersassoc.com. Strong in North America. MSA CSF curriculum was modelled on SMS standards.

Both directories let you search by region. Both vet their listed fitters. Independent fitters with verifiable credentials are also acceptable — ask for their training pathway and check it.

**One caveat about brand-employed fitters.** A fitter employed by a saddle brand (or paid per saddle sold) carries a structural conflict of interest. Many are technically excellent, but the conflict is real. If your only available fitter is brand-employed, treat the fitting as informed advice rather than independent assessment.

---

**This week.** Pull up the SMS or MSA directory and find the two nearest fitters to you. Note their travel range and booking lead time. You don't have to book today — you need to know who you're calling when you need them.
