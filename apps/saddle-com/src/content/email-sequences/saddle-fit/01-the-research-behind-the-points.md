Most saddles that fail a pressure-map check still look fine to the eye.

That sentence is the entire reason this checklist exists.

---

**The instrument that changed saddle fit.** In the early 2000s, two electronic saddle-pad systems — Pliance (Novel GmbH) and Tekscan — made it routine to record what was actually happening between panel and back during work. Researchers could finally measure peak pressure, contact area, pressure symmetry, and dynamic pressure shift gait by gait. Before that, fit was inferred from sweat patterns, hair loss, and behaviour — useful, but lagging indicators.

**What the data showed.**

- Even *visibly* well-fitting saddles routinely produced localised pressures above the capillary-occlusion threshold for sustained contact (commonly cited around 30 kPa). Above that threshold, soft-tissue capillary flow is restricted — pain, then atrophy, then white hairs (Belock B. & Kaiser L., 2012, *Equine Veterinary Journal*).
- Dry patches in the post-ride sweat pattern correspond reliably to those peak-pressure zones (von Peinen K. et al., 2010, *EVJ supplement*). Sweat-pattern reading — point 8 of the checklist — is therefore not folk wisdom; it is the cheapest validated surface indicator we have.
- The same saddle on the same horse produces different pressure distributions under different riders, and different distributions at different gaits. Static fit ≠ dynamic fit. (This is why point 12 — dynamic-fit observation + post-ride hair check — matters even when the static check passes.)

**Why this matters for the 12 points.**

The checklist is not a folk recipe. Each point is doing one of two jobs: ruling out a structural mismatch the rider can see (tree angle, wither clearance, balance, billet hang), or capturing the dynamic / post-ride evidence the rider can read after the fact (sweat pattern, hair pattern, behaviour). The structural points are the *necessary* fit; the evidence points are the *check that the necessary fit is actually delivering*.

---

**One claim I will not make in your inbox.** I will not tell you that the Saddle.com editorial team has personally pressure-mapped saddles. We have not. We are reading and citing the published work — the same work the SMS and MSA reference in their curricula. The advantage of a checklist grounded in cited research, rather than in unverified "we tested" claims, is that you can read the studies yourself. Belock & Kaiser (2012) and von Peinen et al. (2010) are the two starting points.

Schleese, J. (*Suffering In Silence*, Trafalgar Square, 2014) is the practitioner-side reference and is the most widely circulated layperson explanation of the same dataset. He is a saddle-fitter author with a strong point of view — read him alongside the journal sources, not instead of them.

---

**This week.** Use point 8 once. Bring the checklist out to the barn. Walk and trot for ten minutes in your usual saddle. Pull it off. Look at the sweat. Note where the panel was even and where it wasn't. That single observation will change how you read your saddle from then on.
